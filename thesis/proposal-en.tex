\section{Introduction and research problem}

People all over the world are learning to read Arabic to access the Quran in its original language. One
methodology popular in Russia and Kazakhstan is called \emph{al-Muallim ath-Thani} (``The Second Teacher'')
\cite{SRC-040}. It introduces beginners to the letters gradually, one by one, building on Quranic vocabulary.
Learning usually requires a human instructor, yet progress depends very much on how much the learner practises
between classes. We would like to support this out-of-class practice with an AI assistant that checks the
learner's reading and detects mistakes.

Checking reading is more than speech-to-text. Current work treats it as \emph{mispronunciation detection and
diagnosis}: the system knows the text the learner should read, recognises the phonemes actually spoken and
aligns the two to find where they differ \cite{SRC-031}. Several tools already do this. Tarteel highlights
mistakes in recitation, but it is closed, so its accuracy on beginners cannot be verified \cite{SRC-046}.
QVoice gives non-native learners character-level feedback on Arabic pronunciation \cite{SRC-150}. Open models
such as Quran Muaalem recognise phonemes, yet they are trained on expert reciters and their quality drops on
speech with errors: the phoneme error rate (PER) is 0.54\,\% on the authors' test set and 5.78\,\% on
qdat\_bench, which contains real recitation errors \cite{SRC-002}. Whether these tools work for adult
beginners reading primer material is unknown; checking this is the first research question.

\textbf{Problem.} Not everyone can study with a teacher often: work, distance and the cost of lessons get in
the way, and a group class has no time to go through each learner's difficulties. To our knowledge, no
existing dataset or benchmark covers adult beginners learning to read Arabic as a foreign language at the
primer stage, with phoneme-level teacher annotation that separates reading errors from pronunciation errors.
The closest corpus, RetaSy, contains recitations of non-Arabic speakers but records only the reader's
country, not their native language \cite{SRC-032}. Without such data, the quality of existing models on these
learners can be neither measured nor improved.

\section{Research questions}

\textbf{Hypothesis.} A small curated dataset of beginners' speech, annotated by teachers, is enough to show
where existing models fail on these learners and, through model adaptation and agentic pipeline
optimization, to build a more accurate error detection tool for out-of-class practice.

\begin{rqbox}
\textbf{RQ-1.} How accurately do existing Arabic and Quranic speech models detect and locate the errors of
adult beginners: (a) reading errors and (b) pronunciation errors?\\[4pt]
\textbf{RQ-2.} Under a fixed experimental budget, to what extent does agentic pipeline optimization, which
searches for and combines configurations of existing models and components, improve error detection
compared with predefined baseline configurations?
\end{rqbox}

\textbf{What we check.} Recitation errors were classified long before speech models. The classical
tradition separates a \emph{clear error} (lahn jali), which changes a letter or a vowel, from a \emph{hidden
error} (lahn khafi), which breaks a letter's attribute or a tajweed rule \cite{SRC-153}. In an empirical study
of students' recitation, errors of makhraj and letter attributes (sifat) formed the largest group, and qalqala
was the most frequent error within it \cite{SRC-152}. Speech benchmarks either leave tajweed out
\cite{SRC-004} or encode all of it \cite{SRC-002}. \emph{Al-Muallim ath-Thani} starts with letters and vowel
signs and later teaches basic tajweed. We list everything the book teaches and fix in advance what is checked:

A \emph{reading error} means the wrong sign was read: another vowel, a missed sukun or shadda, a
look-alike letter. A \emph{pronunciation error} means the sign was read correctly but the letter was produced
from the wrong makhraj \cite{SRC-059} or with the wrong attribute, for example \ar{ح} pronounced like
\ar{ه}, or a qalqala letter with sukun without its echo. The two can sound the same (\ar{ق} read as \ar{ك} may
be either), so the annotation records what was heard and whether the error is clear or hidden, and the
teacher adds the cause.

\textbf{Method.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Data:} a Telegram bot built on the lessons of \emph{al-Muallim ath-Thani} collects recordings
    with participants' consent; pilot with 20--40 adults over 4--6 weeks. Two teachers annotate each
    recording phoneme by phoneme: the expected sign, what was heard (substitution, deletion, insertion
    or a wrong attribute) and its position; for each error, clear or hidden, and the cause. Learners are split into training, development and test sets, so no learner appears in two
    sets.
  \item \emph{Baselines}, fixed in advance: (B0) a Quran speech-to-text model whose transcript is compared
    with the reference text; (B1) the IQRA 2026 phoneme recognition baseline \cite{SRC-031,SRC-066};
    (B2) a Harf-Speech-style pipeline: phonetizer, speech-to-phoneme model and alignment \cite{SRC-149};
    (B3) Quran Muaalem as is \cite{SRC-002}.
  \item \emph{Agentic pipeline optimization:} a coding agent repeatedly changes the pipeline, evaluates it
    on the development set and keeps what improves it \cite{SRC-067}. It searches over the speech model,
    phoneme representation, alignment, decision thresholds, a verifier and adaptation of models on the
    training learners (such as LoRA fine-tuning \cite{SRC-111}). The number of runs is fixed in advance, the
    agent has no access to the test set, and every configuration tried is logged: without a held-out set,
    agents tend to maximise the metric by memorising the data \cite{SRC-067}.
  \item \emph{Evaluation:} the best configuration is compared with the baselines once, on the frozen test
    set. The main metric is the F1 score of error detection against the teachers' annotation, as in
    IQRA 2026 \cite{SRC-031}. We also report precision, recall, false rejections (correct reading flagged as
    wrong), false acceptances (missed errors) and PER, for reading and pronunciation errors separately and
    for isolated letters, syllables, words and Quranic text: isolated letters are much harder for
    off-the-shelf models \cite{SRC-151}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Novelty and contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Dataset:} a curated speech dataset of adult beginners learning to read Arabic as a foreign
    language at the primer stage, with the learners' native language and phoneme-level teacher
    annotation. The annotation scheme combines the classical clear/hidden distinction with the cause of
    the error: reading or pronunciation (makhraj and sifat).
  \item \emph{Benchmark:} measured accuracy of existing Arabic and Quranic speech models on real beginner
    speech, by error type and by level (letter, syllable, word, Quranic text).
  \item \emph{Pipeline:} an error detection pipeline assembled by agentic pipeline optimization, its gain
    over the baselines on learners it has never seen, and the log of all configurations tried.
  \item \emph{Tool:} a Telegram bot that uses the pipeline to support practice between classes.
\end{enumerate}

\section{State of the art}

Quran Muaalem recognises phonemes and articulatory attributes of Quranic recitation \cite{SRC-002}. The
QuranMB and IQRA 2026 benchmarks address Arabic mispronunciation detection \cite{SRC-004,SRC-031}. In
IQRA 2026 the best system reached $F1=0.72$ against 0.44 for the baseline, and systems that used real
mispronounced speech, only 1,333 utterances or about 1.5 hours, outperformed those relying on synthetic errors
alone \cite{SRC-031}; this supports our bet on a small curated dataset. Harf-Speech assembles the whole
pipeline from the reference text to phoneme alignment, and its scores correlate with the ratings of speech
therapists at $r=0.79$ \cite{SRC-149}. QVoice already offers non-native learners pronunciation practice with
feedback \cite{SRC-150}, so a practice tool by itself is not new. On isolated Arabic letters, an off-the-shelf
wav2vec~2.0 model reaches only 35\,\% accuracy \cite{SRC-151}. The closest corpus of non-native readers,
RetaSy, holds about 7,000 recordings from more than 11 countries \cite{SRC-032}. Automatic pronunciation
feedback has a medium positive effect ($g=0.69$) \cite{SRC-025}, but on accented speech it produces false
alarms, so the teacher stays in the loop \cite{SRC-026}. None of these works targets adult beginners at the
primer stage or separates reading errors from pronunciation errors in its annotation.

\section{Goal of the thesis}

Collect a small curated speech dataset of beginners' primer reading, annotated by teachers, and evaluate
existing models on it. Use agentic pipeline optimization to assemble from these models a reading and
pronunciation error detection system for out-of-class practice, and measure how close it comes to the
teacher.
