\begin{keybox}[Summary]
Adult Russian-speaking Muslims learn to read the Qur'an with Ahmad-Hadi Maksudi's primer
\emph{al-Muallim al-Thani} (``The Second Teacher''), and no teacher can listen to every syllable of every
learner. Open Qur'anic recitation models are trained on expert reciters: they make mistakes on beginners and
know nothing about what a learner has already mastered. This thesis builds a \textbf{dataset} and an
\textbf{AI model}. A tutor that walks the learner through the primer page by page serves as the collection
tool: with consent it gathers the first corpus of primer-level reading by adult Russian-speaking beginners,
and two teachers annotate it at the phoneme level, separating \emph{reading errors} (the wrong sign was read)
from \emph{pronunciation errors} (the sign was right, the sound was wrong). On this corpus the open Quran
Muaalem model \emph{learns} from the teachers, and on top of it a \emph{mastery model} estimates from speech
whether the learner has mastered each skill of the book. The idea is ``one primer, two learners'': the
book's lessons define a skill map along which both the AI and the person learn. The thesis answers four
questions: how reliably the two error types can be told apart (RQ-1); how the AI learns from teacher
corrections and the book's minimal pairs (RQ-2); whether mastery of each skill can be measured from speech
and whether adapting the AI improves that measurement (RQ-3); and how Russian-speaking adults progress
through the book's skills (RQ-4).
\end{keybox}

\section{Problem}

To learn to read the Qur'an, an adult raised with Cyrillic must master a new script and new sounds. The
traditional path is a primer and a teacher: the teacher says a syllable, the learner repeats it, the
teacher corrects at once. This oral transmission, \emph{talaqqi}, works well but scales poorly: a teacher
hears one learner at a time, and many learners live far from a madrasa or study after work.

Automatic checking could be a listener between lessons, but off-the-shelf models do not fit a beginner for
two reasons. First, they are trained on expert reciters and make mistakes on beginners' reading: they
reject correct reading and miss real errors. Second, they score one attempt and do not answer the
teacher's main question: \emph{what has the learner mastered, and what not yet}. Without a dataset of
beginners' reading one can neither measure the first problem, nor fix it, nor build the second capability.
In the words of the author's professor: what can be measured can be solved.

\section{Motivation}

For more than a century one primer has dominated Russia and Central Asia. Ahmad-Hadi Maksudi (1868--1941)
wrote \emph{al-Muallim al-Thani} as a bridge from knowing the letters to reading the Qur'an correctly. The
book follows the Jadid phonetic method: a letter is learned as a sound rather than by its name, and syllables
are gradually built into words \cite{SRC-015,SRC-016,SRC-017}. It is still reprinted, and the Spiritual
Administrations of Muslims of Tatarstan and Kazakhstan officially distribute it \cite{SRC-020,SRC-040}.

Meanwhile, speech technology has learned to listen at the phoneme level. The open Quran Muaalem model
(2025) recognises phonemes together with ten articulatory attributes (\emph{sifat}) and errs on 0.54\,\% of
phonemes on expert recitation \cite{SRC-002}. A meta-analysis finds a medium positive effect of automatic
pronunciation feedback ($g=0.69$), strongest for individual sounds \cite{SRC-025}. Tutors that listen to
children reading aloud have existed for years: Project LISTEN \cite{SRC-125}, DART \cite{SRC-130,SRC-131},
Lalilo \cite{SRC-132,SRC-133}.

The obstacle is just as clear. On qdat\_bench, which contains real mistakes, Muaalem's phoneme error rate
rises to 5.78\,\% \cite{SRC-002}. The best system in the IQRA 2026 challenge reaches a phoneme F1 of 0.72,
and the organisers note that the data contain no learners of Arabic as a second language
\cite{SRC-031,SRC-086}; the QuranMB authors list collecting such data as future work \cite{SRC-004}. On
accented speech, automatic systems produce false alarms, so reviews advise keeping the teacher in the loop
\cite{SRC-026}.

\section{Research gap}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{No data.} Qur'anic assessment sets (QuranMB, Iqra'Eval, IQRA 2026) contain fluent verse
    recitation \cite{SRC-004,SRC-043,SRC-031}. The closest non-Arab corpus, RetaSy, has no Russian speakers
    \cite{SRC-032}, and no corpus separates reading errors from pronunciation errors.
  \item \textbf{No adaptation to adult beginners.} Open Qur'anic models are trained on experts
    \cite{SRC-002}; self-supervised speech models have been adapted to children learning to read
    \cite{SRC-132,SRC-133} and to language learners \cite{SRC-116}, but not to adult non-Arabs learning to
    read Arabic script.
  \item \textbf{No speech-based mastery estimation that accounts for model errors.} Knowledge tracing
    estimates whether a skill is mastered from a sequence of attempts \cite{SRC-124,SRC-126}. Project LISTEN
    applied it to noisy speech recogniser output \cite{SRC-125}, but the recogniser's errors were not
    measured against teachers or modelled explicitly. Educational systems are rarely evaluated by mastery at
    all \cite{SRC-135,SRC-134}.
  \item \textbf{No data on how Russian-speaking adults acquire Arabic reading.} Typical difficulties
    (pharyngeals, emphatics, interdentals, vowel length, gemination) are described in teaching sources and
    for other first languages \cite{SRC-023,SRC-034,SRC-035}, but not measured skill by skill.
\end{enumerate}

Gap 3 was established by several searches rather than a systematic review; a systematic check is planned
before the topic is approved.

\section{Aim, objectives and research questions}

\textbf{Aim.} To develop a dataset of primer-level reading by Russian-speaking beginners and an AI model that
detects reading errors in it and estimates from speech the mastery of each skill of \emph{al-Muallim
al-Thani}, and to measure how close the model comes to a teacher and what it reveals about how people learn.

\textbf{Objectives.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=O\arabic*.]
  \item Turn the primer into a digital curriculum in the book's order, with every exercise traceable to the
    page and line of an edition \cite{SRC-040}, and derive a skill map from it (letter + vowel or sign
    $\to$ sound).
  \item Build a tutor as the data-collection tool: it receives a voice message, recognises phonemes,
    explains the error in Russian and keeps a full decision trace for every attempt.
  \item Collect the dataset: consent (including model training), a pilot with 20--40 learners over 4--6 weeks,
    phoneme-level annotation with error type by two teachers, blind annotation of test learners, a teacher
    test at three points, a datasheet \cite{SRC-123}.
  \item Adapt the recognition model on teacher corrections and the book's minimal pairs (B1, B2) and evaluate
    it on a frozen learner-disjoint test set.
  \item Build a mastery model for the book's skills that accounts for the recogniser's measured errors, and
    validate it on next attempts and against the teacher test.
  \item Describe learners' progress through the book's skills and answer RQ-1--RQ-4 with effect sizes and
    confidence intervals under a preregistered plan.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{rqbox}
\textbf{RQ-1 (data).} How reliably can teachers and the model distinguish reading errors from pronunciation
errors in beginners reading the book?\\[4pt]
\textbf{RQ-2 (how the AI learns).} How much does adaptation on teacher corrections and the book's minimal
pairs improve recognition for new learners, and how does this depend on the amount of data?\\[4pt]
\textbf{RQ-3 (measuring mastery).} Can recognition be used to estimate whether a learner has mastered each
skill of the book, and does adapting the AI improve this estimate?\\[4pt]
\textbf{RQ-4 (how people learn).} How do Russian-speaking adults progress through the book's skills: what
comes quickly, what slowly, what is forgotten?
\end{rqbox}

The hypotheses below are recorded in a preregistration that will be frozen with a SHA-256 fingerprint
before data collection begins \cite{SRC-067}. Everything else is reported as exploratory.

\paragraph{Scope} The curriculum covers the whole book (pp.~10--85), but evaluation focuses on the letter
and vowel lessons (pp.~10--24): learners start there, and they hold the most errors and skills. Tajweed rules
such as \emph{ikhfa} and \emph{idgham}, other first languages, choosing the next card from estimated mastery,
and a controlled experiment on explanations are left for the PhD. The model is adapted, not trained from
scratch: the pilot yields orders of magnitude less data than training from scratch needs. The tutor
complements a teacher and does not claim to replace one. RQ-4 describes how people learn with the system; it
does not prove that the system taught them, which would need a control group.

\section{Approach: an AI solution}

The work follows design science research \cite{SRC-100,SRC-101}: artefacts are built to solve a real
problem, and each is evaluated with a method suited to the specific claim. There are three artefacts
(Fig.~\ref{fig:design-en}).

\subsection{Artefact A: the dataset and the collection tool}

The tutor already works (version 0.6.0). Its curriculum is the whole book --- 125 lessons and 800 cards in
nine parts --- in the book's order: from hamza and the letter group \ar{ر ز م} on p.~10 to the short surahs at
the end. Every card cites the page and line of the 2010 Almetyevsk edition, and 139 Qur'anic examples were
checked against the Madinah mushaf \cite{SRC-040,SRC-041}.

The learner opens a card, may listen to a reference and sends a voice message. The pipeline
(Fig.~\ref{fig:pipe-en}) converts the recording, recognises phonemes, aligns them with the line's canonical
phonemes and assigns each mismatch an error type and a syllable. Every attempt stores the reference, the
heard phonemes, the raw model output with per-phoneme confidence, the thresholds, and the code, curriculum
and model versions --- so any verdict can be recomputed with another model. Audio is kept only with explicit
revocable consent; learners are pseudonymised; one button deletes all of one's data.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\resizebox{\linewidth}{!}{\input{figures/fig-pipeline}}
\caption{One attempt: from a voice message to an explanation and a dataset record.}
\label{fig:pipe-en}
\end{figure}

Two teachers annotate attempts at the phoneme level. What is new is the \textbf{error type}. A \emph{reading
error}: the wrong sign was read --- another vowel, a missed sukun, shadda or madd, letters that look alike
(\ar{ب}, \ar{ت} and \ar{ث}). A \emph{pronunciation error}: the sign was read correctly but the sound was produced
wrongly (\ar{ح} as Russian ``kh'', \ar{ق} as ``k'', no emphasis). Unclear cases are labelled ``unclear''. For
learning these are different problems: the first needs a hint about the sign, the second about articulation.
The corpus comes with a datasheet \cite{SRC-123}.

\subsection{Artefact B: a recognition model that learns from teachers}

Three systems are compared on the same recordings. \textbf{B0} is Muaalem as is, with rules and thresholds.
\textbf{B1} is a verifier trained on top of B0's outputs: for each phoneme it takes the model's confidence,
the articulatory-attribute probabilities, \emph{Goodness of Pronunciation} \cite{SRC-115}, duration and
material type, and estimates the probability of a real error. Its probabilities are calibrated
\cite{SRC-112}, and the threshold is chosen by conformal risk control so that the false-rejection rate stays
below a chosen $\varepsilon$ \cite{SRC-113,SRC-114}; where confidence is insufficient, the outcome is
``not sure'' \cite{SRC-117} and the attempt goes to a teacher. \textbf{B2} is Muaalem fine-tuned with LoRA
\cite{SRC-111} on teacher-corrected phonemes \cite{SRC-116}. The book itself supplies more errors to learn
from: a correct reading of the card with \ar{ك} is a ready-made error example for the paired card with
\ar{ق}, and so on for every minimal pair \cite{SRC-119}; reading errors are simulated similarly for children
\cite{SRC-132}. There are three data sources --- teacher corrections, the book's minimal pairs and learners'
``the bot was wrong'' disputes --- and the contribution of each is measured. The fine-tuned model is also
tested on expert recitation, so that gains are not bought by forgetting \cite{SRC-118}.

\subsection{Artefact C: a mastery model for the book's skills}

The book's lessons are a ready-made \emph{skill map}: each exercise introduces ``letter + vowel or sign
$\to$ sound'' correspondences in a fixed order, and each line of the book exercises several such skills.
Along this map a mastery model tracks whether the learner has mastered each skill. \textbf{K0} is the share
of correct attempts per skill. \textbf{K1} is Bayesian knowledge tracing \cite{SRC-124}: the probability that
a skill is mastered is updated after each attempt, with guess and slip parameters. \textbf{K2} is the same,
but the ``correct / incorrect'' observation comes from a recogniser that itself errs, and its per-phoneme
false acceptances and rejections, measured against teachers on the training part, enter the model
explicitly. This extends the idea of Project LISTEN \cite{SRC-125}. The models are evaluated by how well they
predict the next attempt and by calibration \cite{SRC-126}; deep knowledge tracing \cite{SRC-127} is only
exploratory on small data. The skill map is checked with learning curves \cite{SRC-128}. In this way the
AI's learning (artefact B) directly affects how accurately we see the person's learning.

\subsection{How validity is protected}

The system is built by one author together with an AI agent (Claude Code). Important rules are enforced by
the environment rather than by the agent's goodwill \cite{SRC-029,SRC-076}: hooks forbid editing tests in the
implementation phase and reading personal data, require human approval in human-owned zones, and do not let
the agent finish until architecture contracts, type checks and tests pass. The same mechanisms protect the
experiments: the list of test learners and the evaluator are frozen with a SHA-256 fingerprint, and the agent
may not read test labels --- following rules R1--R5 from the supervisor's group \cite{SRC-067}. Whether this
methodology deserves its own research question or a separate paper will be decided with the supervisor.

\section{Studies}

\paragraph{Study 1 --- data (RQ-1)} Both teachers annotate phonemes and error type on all test attempts and
on every tenth training attempt. The reliability of the error type is Cohen's $\kappa$ \cite{SRC-104}
between teachers, with ``unclear'' as its own category. The model is compared with teachers following the
Iqra'Eval and L2-ARCTIC protocols \cite{SRC-063,SRC-064}: precision, recall, F1, false acceptances (FAR) and
rejections (FRR), and $\kappa$ ``model -- teacher'' against the ``teacher -- teacher'' ceiling; phoneme-level
agreement is Krippendorff's $\alpha$ \cite{SRC-065}. Confidence intervals are bootstrapped over learners.

\paragraph{Study 2 --- how the AI learns (RQ-2)} Before any training, about 30\,\% of learners are assigned
to the test set and their list is fingerprinted; none of their recordings is used for training, calibration
or threshold selection \cite{SRC-122}. Both teachers annotate test learners' attempts \emph{blind}, without
seeing the system's answer, and reconcile disagreements --- this is the gold standard. Hyperparameters are
chosen only by learner-level cross-validation within the training part \cite{SRC-121}. The test set is opened
once. The summary measure is the \emph{gap closed}: how far a system's $\kappa$ with gold rises from B0
toward the teacher--teacher ceiling. The \emph{learning curve} (quality at 25, 50, 75 and 100\,\% of training
learners), the \emph{contribution of each data source}, and the share of attempts the system decides on its
own at a given risk --- the workload it takes off the teacher --- are always reported.

\paragraph{Study 3 --- measuring mastery (RQ-3)} Parameters of K1 and K2, and the recogniser's errors for
K2, are estimated on training learners. On test learners the models predict the gold outcome of the next
attempt on a skill; AUC, log loss and calibration are compared for K0, K1 and K2 on top of B0 and on top of
the adapted model. The external criterion is the teacher test: from a fixed list of book lines the teacher
marks each skill ``correct / incorrect'' without seeing the system's estimates; the final mastery estimate is
compared with it by Spearman's $\rho$.

\paragraph{Study 4 --- how people learn (RQ-4)} The teacher test is given three times: at entry, at the end of
the pilot and 1--2 weeks later. From the logs we build learning curves for the book's skills
\cite{SRC-128}, attempts to mastery and the drop after breaks between sessions \cite{SRC-129}; skills with
sounds absent from Russian are compared with the rest in a mixed model with a learner random effect
\cite{SRC-106}. Following DART \cite{SRC-130,SRC-131}, the AI's accuracy and people's learning are reported
separately. Holm's correction is applied across the twelve confirmatory hypotheses.

\section{Expected contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item[\textbf{C1.}] \textbf{Dataset:} the first corpus of reading by adult Russian-speaking beginners, tied to
    the skills of \emph{al-Muallim al-Thani}, separating reading from pronunciation errors, with measured
    annotation reliability, a blind gold test set and a datasheet; released with the weights if ethics and
    consent allow \cite{SRC-097,SRC-123}.
  \item[\textbf{C2.}] \textbf{A model that learns from teachers:} small-data adaptation of an open model (a
    verifier with a guarantee on the false-rejection rate and LoRA with the book's minimal pairs) with a
    learning curve and the measured contribution of each data source.
  \item[\textbf{C3.}] \textbf{Mastery estimation from speech:} a mastery model that accounts for recogniser
    errors measured against teachers, validated on next attempts and against a blind teacher test.
  \item[\textbf{C4.}] \textbf{Knowledge about learners:} the first learning curves of Russian-speaking adults
    over the skills of a primer --- what comes quickly, what slowly, what is forgotten.
\end{enumerate}

The engineering result is a reproducible ``collect $\to$ annotate $\to$ train $\to$ evaluate'' pipeline with
replaceable model, database and messenger --- a ready testbed for comparing models.

\paragraph{Foundation for the PhD} The master's thesis leaves the doctoral work its tools: the dataset, the
frozen test set, the skill map, the mastery model and the training pipeline. This sets up the doctoral line
``teaching the AI and learning with it'': choosing the next card from estimated mastery (an extension of OBER
\cite{SRC-134}), choosing which attempts to send to a teacher for annotation, and transferring the model to
other first languages and tajweed rules.

\section{Ethics, data protection and religious sensitivity}

Voice recordings combined with a religious subject make the data sensitive. Consent to use the service is
therefore separate from consent to voice recording; no audio is kept without consent; all reports and exports
use pseudonyms only; only adults may take part; one button deletes one's data \cite{SRC-033,SRC-036,SRC-037}.
The open questions are institutional: who is the data controller, whether the university ethics committee
must approve, and where the server is located. They will be settled with the supervisor before anyone outside
the closed circle is invited. The tutor uses only the Arabic mushaf text and its own neutral explanations and
does not reproduce the Russian text of the book. The consent explicitly covers model training, release of the
weights and the teacher tests; no voiceprints are built. Muaalem and its base w2v-BERT~2.0 are MIT-licensed
\cite{SRC-003}.

\section{Current status and preliminary evidence}

The whole system works end to end with the real model. In a first check on the teaching recitation of
Mahmoud Khalil al-Husary \cite{SRC-042}, correct verses scored 99--100, and deliberately mismatched ones scored
40--67: on expert recitation the pipeline tells correct from incorrect reading. On the first nine attempts the
median processing time was 1.7\,s (90th percentile 5.0\,s). The properties of the scoring algorithm were
checked on 5{,}000 random phoneme strings per property with no violations \cite{SRC-096}. The code has about
9{,}000 lines of Python in 92 modules; 736 automated tests pass; 47 functional requirements are traced to
tests. Every attempt already stores the raw model output with per-phoneme confidence, the model version and
the time --- exactly what B1, system comparison and the mastery model need. None of these numbers answers a
research question yet; they show that the instrument is ready.
