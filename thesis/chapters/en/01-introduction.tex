\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:intro}

\section{Context and relevance}

Reading the Qur'an aloud is a daily practice for many Muslims, and correct pronunciation is part of the
practice itself. For Arabic speakers the main task is to learn the rules of recitation; for everyone else
the task starts much earlier, with the letters and the sounds behind them. In Russia and Kazakhstan this
path has a long and specific history. At the end of the nineteenth century the Jadid reformers replaced
the old method, in which pupils memorised the names of letters for years, with a sound-based method that
taught a letter as a sound and built syllables from it in small steps \cite{SRC-016,SRC-017}. Ahmad Hadi
Maksudi's primer \emph{al-Muallim ath-Thani} (``The Second Teacher'') brought this method to the reading of the
Qur'an \cite{SRC-015,SRC-018}. More than a century later it is still printed by the Muslim board of Tatarstan and
officially distributed in Kazakhstan \cite{SRC-040,SRC-020}. Today \emph{The Second Teacher} is a popular
method for teaching Arabic reading in Russia and Kazakhstan: beginners use it to learn to read the Qur'an from
scratch.

The primer has never been used without a teacher. A lesson follows the cycle of \emph{talaqqi}: the
teacher reads, the learner repeats, the teacher corrects every slip, and the learner moves on only after an
error-free reading \cite{SRC-021,SRC-058}. The cycle is established in the teaching tradition, but it ties every learner to a teacher's
time. Adults who study after work, live far from a mosque school or feel shy reading in front of others
practise alone between lessons, and nobody tells them when a \ar{ح} has turned into a Russian [x] or a long
vowel has become short.

Two developments make it reasonable to ask whether a machine could fill part of that gap. Automatic speech
recognition has reached the level of phonemes for Qur'anic Arabic: the open model Quran Muaalem recognises
phonemes together with ten articulatory attributes and reports a phoneme error rate of about half a percent
on expert recitations \cite{SRC-002}. And tutors that listen to reading aloud already work for children
learning to read their native language \cite{SRC-125,SRC-130,SRC-132}; automatic pronunciation feedback has a
medium positive effect, largest when it targets individual sounds \cite{SRC-025}.

The topic is relevant for research, for practice and for industry. For research, open phoneme-level models
for Qur'anic Arabic appeared only recently \cite{SRC-002}, and public benchmarks contain no learners of Arabic as
a second language; the benchmark authors themselves name collecting such data as future work
\cite{SRC-004,SRC-031}. For practice, the primer is still printed and officially distributed
\cite{SRC-040,SRC-020}, so a tool built around it serves an existing teaching tradition instead of asking it to
change. For the speech-technology and educational-software industry, the thesis addresses a problem every team
faces when it deploys speech recognition for a group the model was not trained on: how to adapt an open model on
small data collected inside a working product and how to evaluate it without leakage.

The thesis belongs to the field of study 09.04.01 ``Computer Science'', profile Software Engineering: its
artefacts are a dataset, an error-detection system and the software that collects and processes the data; requirements are written
as quality-attribute scenarios, the architecture is checked mechanically, and the training and evaluation
pipeline is built to be reproducible.

\section{Problem}

An off-the-shelf model does not fit a beginner for two reasons. The first is who is speaking. Muaalem was
trained only on male expert reciters. On recordings with real errors its phoneme error rate is ten times higher
than on its own test set \cite{SRC-002}. Public benchmarks for Qur'anic pronunciation contain fluent verse
recitation and no learners of Arabic as a second language \cite{SRC-004,SRC-031,SRC-043}. Recognisers produce
false alarms on accented speech \cite{SRC-026}. A Russian-speaking adult reading a single syllable slowly for
the first time is far from the model's training data, and nobody knows how often the model will be wrong about
such a learner or whether it can be adapted to them. The methods exist --- low-rank fine-tuning
\cite{SRC-111}, calibration and abstention \cite{SRC-112,SRC-117} --- but they need data from the new
population, and there are none.

The second reason is what the model sees. It scores one attempt, while a teacher cares about something else:
\emph{what the learner has already mastered}. Knowledge tracing can answer that question from a sequence of
attempts \cite{SRC-124}, including from noisy recogniser output \cite{SRC-125}, but recogniser errors measured
against teachers have not been built into such models, and nobody has built them for adults learning to read
Arabic script.

Both reasons come down to one thing: there is no dataset. Without it one cannot measure how wrong off-the-shelf
models are on beginners learning Arabic as a foreign language, cannot build a more accurate system from them,
and cannot check whether it estimates mastery correctly. The supervisor puts it
this way: what can be measured can be solved.

\section{Aim and objectives}

The aim is \emph{to collect a small curated speech dataset of primer-level reading by beginners annotated by
teachers, to evaluate existing speech recognition and analysis models on it, and to use agentic loop-engineering
to build from them a reading and pronunciation error-detection system for an out-of-class practice tool};
additionally, \emph{to assess how the tool affects learning dynamics.}

Objectives:
\begin{enumerate}[label=O\arabic*.]
  \item turn the primer into a digital curriculum in the book's own order, with every exercise traceable to a
    page and line of the edition, and derive a skill map from it;
  \item build a tutor as the data-collection tool: it recognises phonemes, explains errors in Russian for a
    learner starting from zero and keeps a full decision trace for every attempt;
  \item collect the dataset: consent, including consent to model training, and pseudonyms; a closed pilot with
    20--40 learners over 4--6 weeks; phoneme-level and error-type annotation by two teachers; a teacher test at three points; a datasheet;
  \item evaluate existing speech recognition and analysis models against teacher annotation;
  \item build agentic loop-engineering, in which an AI agent selects and combines off-the-shelf models and
    components (including a fine-tuned model) by cross-validation on the training part, and compare the best
    configuration once with baseline approaches on a frozen test set of new learners;
  \item additionally, build a mastery model for the book's skills that accounts for the recogniser's measured
    errors and, with it and the teacher test, describe learning dynamics with the tool;
  \item answer the research questions with effect sizes and confidence intervals under a preregistered plan.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Hypothesis and research questions}\label{sec:rq}

\begin{rqbox}
\textbf{Main hypothesis.} By collecting a small curated speech dataset, it is possible to evaluate existing solutions and, using agentic loop-engineering, develop an effective tool for beginners' out-of-class practice.\\[6pt]
\textbf{RQ-1.} How reliably do existing speech recognition and analysis models detect reading and pronunciation errors in beginners learning Arabic and Quran reading?\\[4pt]
\textbf{RQ-2.} To what extent does applying agentic loop-engineering to select and combine existing models and components improve the accuracy of reading and pronunciation error detection compared with baseline approaches?\\[4pt]
\textbf{RQ-3 (additional).} To what extent does using the developed tool for out-of-class practice affect the learning dynamics of beginners studying with a teacher?
\end{rqbox}

The questions form a chain. RQ-1 checks what can be relied on: whether the annotation is reliable and how far
off-the-shelf models are from a teacher. RQ-2 measures how much agentic loop-engineering, building a system from
the same off-the-shelf models and components, narrows this gap compared with baseline approaches. RQ-3, an
additional question, tests the tool on people: how mastery of the material changes for learners who study with
a teacher and practise with the tool outside lessons. Confirmatory hypotheses for RQ-1 are listed in
Section~\ref{sec:method-hyp}; hypotheses for RQ-2 and RQ-3 are formulated with the supervisor before the
preregistration is frozen; everything else is reported as exploratory.

\section{Approach}

The thesis follows design science research \cite{SRC-100,SRC-101}. It builds three artefacts, and each claim is
evaluated with a suitable method. The dataset with its collection tool (Artefact A) is evaluated by measuring
annotation reliability, and the reliability of off-the-shelf models is measured on it. The error-detection
system built by agentic loop-engineering (Artefact B) is evaluated by a controlled experiment against baseline
approaches on a frozen test set of new learners. The mastery model (Artefact C) serves, together with the
teacher test, as the instrument for measuring learning dynamics, which are described longitudinally.
Requirements are written as quality-attribute scenarios \cite{SRC-047}, metrics are derived with the
Goal--Question--Metric method \cite{SRC-048}, and the analysis plan and data split will be preregistered and
fingerprinted before research data collection and before the agentic loop first runs. The system is built by
the author with an AI agent under mechanical checks that protect both the code and the experiments
\cite{SRC-067}.

\section{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item[\textbf{C1.}] \textbf{Dataset.} The first dataset of primer-level reading by beginners learning Arabic as a
    foreign language, tied to the skills of \emph{al-Muallim ath-Thani}, collected with consent, annotated at the
    phoneme level and by error type by two teachers, with measured reliability, a gold test set and a
    datasheet; released with the weights if ethics and consent allow.
  \item[\textbf{C2.}] \textbf{Evaluation of off-the-shelf models.} The reliability of open speech recognition and
    analysis models on beginners, measured against teachers: precision, recall, F1, false acceptance and
    rejection rates, and ``model -- teacher'' against ``teacher -- teacher'' agreement.
  \item[\textbf{C3.}] \textbf{A system built by agentic loop-engineering.} A configuration of off-the-shelf models
    and components (a calibrated verifier, LoRA fine-tuning with augmentation from the book's minimal pairs),
    its gain over baseline approaches on a frozen test set, and a log of every configuration tried.
  \item[\textbf{C4.}] \textbf{Additionally --- learning dynamics.} How mastery of the material changes with the
    tool, by a mastery model that accounts for recogniser errors and by the teacher test.
\end{enumerate}

The engineering result is a reproducible ``collect $\to$ annotate $\to$ agentic loop $\to$ evaluate'' pipeline
with replaceable models, database and messenger.

\section{Object of research and novelty}\label{sec:novelty}

The \emph{object of research} is a speech dataset of primer-level reading by beginners learning Arabic as a
foreign language and a reading and pronunciation error-detection system built from off-the-shelf models; the
Telegram tutor is the instrument that collects the data, not the object. The \emph{subject of research} is how
accurately off-the-shelf models and a system built by agentic loop-engineering detect reading and pronunciation
errors compared with teachers.

The novelty of the thesis, as far as the review in Chapter~\ref{ch:review} shows, is as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item the dataset (C1) is the first collection of primer-level reading by beginners learning Arabic as a
    foreign language tied to the skills of a primer, and its annotation separates reading errors from
    pronunciation errors;
  \item the reliability of open Qur'anic recitation models is measured on beginners against teachers for the
    first time (C2);
  \item the error-detection system is built by agentic loop-engineering from off-the-shelf models and
    components, with a log of every configuration tried and a test set protected from overfitting (C3)
    \pending{check the novelty of agentic loop-engineering for this task in the review, Chapter~\ref{ch:review}};
  \item additionally, learning dynamics with the tool are described by a mastery model that accounts for
    recogniser errors measured against teachers (C4).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope and limitations}

The curriculum covers the whole book (pp.~10--85), but evaluation focuses on the letter and vowel lessons
(pp.~10--24): learners start there, and they hold the most errors and skills. Tajweed rules such as
\emph{ikhfa} and \emph{idgham}, other first languages, choosing the next card from estimated mastery and a
controlled experiment on explanations are left for future work, as are the earlier questions on how the AI
learns, how to measure mastery and how people learn (their strength is to be re-checked). Models are not trained
from scratch: agentic loop-engineering selects, combines and, with enough data, fine-tunes off-the-shelf models.
Without a control group, RQ-3 describes learning dynamics with the tool but does not prove that the tool caused
them. The
tutor is meant to complement a teacher; the thesis does not claim that it can replace one --- and the book itself
says it cannot.

\section{Structure of the thesis}

Chapter~\ref{ch:review} reviews the primer and its method, the learners, research on pronunciation feedback,
automatic assessment of Qur'anic recitation, methods for adapting speech models, reading tutors and knowledge
tracing, and software engineering with AI agents. Chapter~\ref{ch:method} sets out the methodology:
requirements, metrics, three studies, preregistration and threats to validity. Chapter~\ref{ch:impl} describes
the implementation --- from the curriculum and skill map to the collection tool, agentic loop-engineering, the
mastery model and the development harness. Chapter~\ref{ch:eval} presents and discusses the evaluation, and
Chapter~\ref{ch:concl} concludes with answers and directions for the PhD.
