\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:intro}

\section{Context}

Reading the Qur'an aloud is a daily practice for many Muslims, and correct pronunciation is part of the
practice itself. For Arabic speakers the main task is to learn the rules of recitation; for everyone else
the task starts much earlier, with the letters and the sounds behind them. In Russia and Central Asia this
path has a long and specific history. At the end of the nineteenth century the Jadid reformers replaced
the old method, in which pupils memorised the names of letters for years, with a sound-based method that
taught a letter as a sound and built syllables from it in small steps \cite{SRC-016,SRC-017}. Ahmad Hadi
Maksudi's primer \emph{Muallim al-Sani} (``The Second Teacher'') brought this method to the reading of the
Qur'an \cite{SRC-015}. More than a century later it is still printed by the Muslim board of Tatarstan and
officially distributed in Kazakhstan \cite{SRC-040,SRC-020}.

The primer has never been used without a teacher. A lesson follows the cycle of \emph{talaqqi}: the
teacher reads, the learner repeats, the teacher corrects every slip, and the learner moves on only after an
error-free reading \cite{SRC-021,SRC-058}. The cycle is effective, but it ties every learner to a teacher's
time. Adults who study after work, live far from a mosque school or feel shy reading in front of others
practise alone between lessons, and nobody tells them when a \ar{ح} has turned into a Russian [x] or a long
vowel has become short.

Two developments make it reasonable to ask whether a machine could fill part of that gap. Automatic speech
recognition has reached the level of phonemes for Qur'anic Arabic: the open model Quran Muaalem recognises
phonemes together with ten articulatory attributes and reports a phoneme error rate of about half a percent
on expert recitations \cite{SRC-002}. And research on computer-assisted pronunciation training has shown
that automatic feedback has a medium positive effect, largest when it targets individual sounds and states
the error explicitly \cite{SRC-025} --- which is exactly what a primer does.

\section{Problem}

The difficulty lies in who is speaking. Muaalem was trained only on male expert reciters. On recordings
with real errors its phoneme error rate is ten times higher than on its own test set, and some rules are
recognised with an F1 below one half \cite{SRC-002}. Public benchmarks for Qur'anic pronunciation contain
fluent recitation of verses and no second-language learners \cite{SRC-004,SRC-031,SRC-043}. Systematic
reviews warn that speech recognisers produce false alarms on accented speech and recommend that a teacher
or partner stay in the loop \cite{SRC-026}. A Russian-speaking adult who reads an isolated syllable slowly
for the first time is far from the model's training data, and nobody knows how often the model will be
wrong about such a learner, how often it will reject a correct reading, or whether its explanations will
help.

A second problem concerns how such a system is built. The software in this thesis was written by one
author with an AI coding agent. Agents are productive, but they are known to make their own tests pass by
changing the tests \cite{SRC-027}, and written instructions lose force over a long session
\cite{SRC-076}. Research software whose numbers end up in a thesis needs stronger guarantees than an
instruction file.

\section{Aim and objectives}

The aim of the thesis is \emph{to design a reading tutor that faithfully follows} Muallim al-Sani,
\emph{and to measure how far an open phoneme-level model can take the role of the listening teacher for
Russian-speaking beginners, without overstating what it can do.}

The objectives are:
\begin{enumerate}[label=O\arabic*.]
  \item to turn the primer into a digital curriculum in the book's own order, with every exercise
    traceable to a page and line of a concrete edition;
  \item to build a tutor that assesses phoneme-level reading, explains errors in Russian for a learner who
    starts from zero, and records a full decision trace for every attempt;
  \item to build the research infrastructure: consent and pseudonyms, phoneme-level annotation by two
    teachers, automated metrics and a preregistered analysis plan;
  \item to run a closed pilot with 20--40 learners over 4--6 weeks, including a within-subject feedback
    experiment and a delayed post-test;
  \item to answer the research questions with effect sizes and confidence intervals;
  \item to document the AI-agent harness used to build the system and evaluate it as a case study.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Research questions}\label{sec:rq}

\begin{rqbox}
\textbf{RQ-1.} How closely does the automatic assessment --- Muaalem, rule-based alignment and grading
thresholds --- agree with qualified teachers on beginners reading \emph{Muallim al-Sani}?\\[4pt]
\textbf{RQ-2.} Which reading errors do Russian-speaking beginners make, and which of the errors reported by
the tutor are errors of the learner rather than of the model?\\[4pt]
\textbf{RQ-3.} Do explicit explanations in Russian improve the next attempt and delayed retention compared
with a bare correct/incorrect signal?\\[4pt]
\textbf{RQ-4.} Which known failure modes of AI-agent development does a mechanically enforced harness
prevent or expose, and how much human intervention does it require?
\end{rqbox}

RQ-1 to RQ-3 concern the tutor; RQ-4 concerns the way it was built. Seven confirmatory hypotheses for
RQ-1 to RQ-3 are listed in Section~\ref{sec:method-hyp}; everything else is reported as exploratory.

\section{Approach}

The thesis follows design science research \cite{SRC-100,SRC-101}. It builds two artefacts and evaluates
each claim with a method suited to it. The tutor (artefact A) is evaluated by a measurement study against
teachers, a descriptive analysis of errors and a pilot experiment. The development harness (artefact B)
is evaluated as an embedded case study \cite{SRC-102}. Requirements are written as quality-attribute
scenarios \cite{SRC-047}, metrics are derived with the Goal--Question--Metric method \cite{SRC-048}, and
the analysis plan is preregistered and fingerprinted before data collection.

\section{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Empirical.} The first teacher-referenced measurement of an open Qur'anic phoneme model on
    primer-level reading by second-language beginners, including how often it wrongly rejects correct
    reading.
  \item \textbf{Data.} A small consented dataset of beginners' readings with phoneme-level labels from two
    teachers, prepared for release in the Iqra'Eval format if ethics and consent allow.
  \item \textbf{Design.} A tutor whose features are derived from Maksudi's didactic principles and from
    feedback research, with an open architecture in which the model, the database and the messenger can be
    replaced without touching domain logic.
  \item \textbf{Method.} An evidence-based description of a guarded AI-agent harness for research software:
    what it prevents, what it costs and where it fails.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope and limitations}

The curriculum covers the whole book (pages 10--85), but the evaluation concentrates on the letter and vowel
lessons (pages 10--24), where beginners start and errors are most frequent. Tajwid rules such as
\emph{ikhfa} and \emph{idgham}, other first languages and fine-tuning of the model are left for future
work. The tutor is meant to complement a teacher; the thesis does not claim that it can replace one, and
the book itself says it cannot.

\section{Structure of the thesis}

Chapter~\ref{ch:review} reviews the primer and its method, the learners, research on pronunciation
feedback, automatic assessment of Qur'anic recitation and AI-agent software engineering.
Chapter~\ref{ch:method} sets out the methodology: requirements, metrics, the four studies, preregistration
and threats to validity. Chapter~\ref{ch:impl} describes the implementation, from the curriculum to the
assessment pipeline, the research data and the development harness. Chapter~\ref{ch:eval} presents and
discusses the evaluation, and Chapter~\ref{ch:concl} concludes with answers and directions for doctoral
research.
