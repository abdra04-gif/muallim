Part 2 · Theory · two tasks

Recognising speech is not the same as checking pronunciation

Speech recognisers (Whisper, Siri, Google) guess words and “fix” mistakes — what a teacher must not do. A check for beginners must report sounds as they were said.

One recording of an Indonesian pupil from the open set. The sound model is right like this in about half of the recordings (part 3).

Part 2 · Theory · what we check

The book goes from one letter to whole lines — the check must follow it

A vowel sign changes the sound: بَ “ba”, بِ “bi”, بُ “bu”. One wrong vowel sign is already a different reading, so the check must see vowel signs.

Part 2 · Theory · why it is hard

Three reasons why ready-made models lose a beginner’s errors

🪄

They “fix” the learner

Recognisers have a built-in dictionary and pick a likely word; a wrong vowel becomes the right word.

Whisper large-v3 on Quran audio: 99 words of 100 wrong once vowel signs count (Grigoryan et al., 2025)

🎓

They hear the rule, not the learner

Models trained only on master reciters never heard a mistake, so they often “hear” the correct sound.

Quran Lab model card (zipformer v3): in 81 of 94 error clips it wrote the expected sound

⚖️

Nobody decides

Models only output sounds. Whether to say “wrong”, “say it again” or nothing is left to the app; most use one threshold.

HAFSS (2006): “false alarms discourage novices” — so it stayed silent when unsure

Part 2 · Theory · four ways

Every system in the world finds errors in one of four ways

Survey A found 10 techniques; they all reduce to these 4. For single letters the best is C, for words B.

Part 2 · Theory · ways B and C in detail

The two ways that work: check the expected sound (B) and pick from a list (C)

B · Check each expected sound

HAFSS, Quran Lab, Azure · for us: words (plan)

  1. From the book we know the expected sounds: ص + a
  2. For each, the usual substitute: instead of ص learners say س
  3. The model gives each a probability: the substitute 0.7, the expected 0.2
  4. The substitute won → “you said plain s instead of emphatic ṣ”

+ names the exact error; works for words and lines

− needs a list of substitutes and a threshold per error type

C · Pick from a short list

children’s letter tools · for us: letters (tested)

  1. All possible answers are known: 28 letters × 3 vowels = 84
  2. The model listens once and gives a “fingerprint” of the sound
  3. A small layer trained on examples gives 84 probabilities
  4. Look at the asked syllable: high → passed, low → error

+ 9 of 10 on our test; trains in minutes; ignores a held vowel

− only for a fixed list (letters, syllables); needs our recordings

A and D: A writes down any sound and compares strings (the bot today) — it loses errors and scolds a held vowel; D compares with a teacher’s voice — tells “how far”, not “what is wrong”.

Part 2 · Theory · comparison

“Lining up the rows” = putting expected and heard sounds one under the other

Like comparing two words letter by letter. Each sound is matched, replaced, missing or extra — and the bot names the error by that: “another vowel”, “sound missing”, “extra sound”. The score drops with each difference; under 80 the bot says “repeat”.

Part 2 · Theory · the best in the world

The only system with numbers on real learners catches fewer than half of their errors

What it means for us

Even the best machine misses half of real errors. So a machine cannot be the final judge: it must ask again when unsure, and teachers must check its answers on our learners.

Part 3 · Experiments · step 2 · main finding

Don’t make the model guess the sound — ask “is this the syllable we asked for?”

Where “9 of 10” comes from

Same model (Arabic phoneme, not Muaalem): correct readings passed 9 of 10 instead of 5 of 10. Per student from 7 to 10 of 10. 48 students, each judged by a check that never heard them.

Why the threshold 0.3

We tried three: 0.1 passes 94 correct of 100 but lets 10 swaps of 100 through; 0.3 — 91 and 5; 0.5 — 87 and 4. 0.3 is the middle; on our own recordings we will choose again.

Part 3 · Experiments · how the new check works

The new check, technically: five steps and three answers

How it is trained

On recordings where we know which syllable was read: the model listens to each once, the small layer learns which fingerprint belongs to which of the 84. On a laptop: 40 min of listening, a few minutes of training.

How it enters the bot

As a second speech model behind the same socket: for letter lessons the bot asks the new check, for words — Muaalem. Screens and explanations stay.

Part 3 · Experiments · hardest letters

On look-alike sounds transcription fails — and the check helps most

Share of recognised readings, ~320 per letter (all 48 students). Mostly emphatic and between-the-teeth sounds that are easy to confuse with plain t, d, s, z, k.

Part 3 · Experiments · where it is wrong

Where the new check is wrong, and how “say it again” helps

It sometimes passes a look-alike letter

How we tested: took a recording of plain س and checked it as if ص had been asked. Did the check say “passed”?

About 5 times in 100 — yes. Worst: ث/س (up to 16 in 100) and ظ (only 59 of 100 correct readings passed).

Without the check (writing down sounds) such a swap passes 13 times in 100.

“Say it again” makes it more accurate

If the check answers only when sure and asks to repeat otherwise:

AnswersRight
always88 of 100
on 88 of 100 recordings93 of 100
on 75 of 100 recordings96 of 100

The price: one learner in four repeats once more.

Part 3 · Experiments · the bot today

We ran the 840 recordings through the bot’s real code: it would say “repeat” to 86 of 100

The bot passes 14 of 100. The new check, on the same 725 “repeat” recordings, passes 629, asks again on 37 and names an error on 59.

What “the bot’s code” is

The same modules that answer learners in Telegram: Muaalem model → two references (muaalem.py) → comparison (assessment.py) → score and verdict (policy.py). Only the recordings were someone else’s.

What it gave and how we use it

We saw how the bot behaves on letters before real learners did, and found 2 methodology errors. The same script (demo_data.py) will run on our labelled recordings: then we will know how often the bot agrees with the teacher.

Part 3 · Experiments · why “repeat”

The commonest reason is a held vowel — normal when saying one letter

What to fix

Held vowel: do not judge length on single letters — in 204 recordings it is the only reason, they would pass at once. Another letter: use the new check for letters. Several sounds replaced: keep — these look like real errors; teachers will confirm.

Number of “repeat” recordings (725 of 840) naming each reason; one recording can have several, so the sum is larger.

Task 2 — the answer

2

Try the best on recordings

Implement the best candidates and see how they work on recordings.

What we compared

5 ready systems on 840 recordings; then 4 ways of deciding on top of one model, on 8,904 recordings of 48 students; and the bot’s own code.

The best

Pick 1 of 84 on top of the Arabic phoneme model: 9 correct readings of 10 passed (free transcription 5, the bot 1.4).

Technical difference

Others write a string of sounds and compare it letter by letter: one wrong or extra sound breaks the score. Ours asks one question with 84 known answers and looks at the whole sound at once — a held vowel does not matter.

Limits

Tested on other people’s students; about 5 swaps in 100 pass. On our learners — after our recordings.

Part 4 · Our bot · one real recording

A student, it seems, held the vowel: the bot said “repeat” (67 < 80), the new check passed it

Why 67 and why 80

Score = share of matching sounds: 2 matched of 2 expected + 4 heard → 2×2 / 6 = 67. Pass mark 80 was set by the author in the bot’s rules (policy.py), not tuned on data; 90+ without errors = “excellent”.

The new check on the same voice

Another model (MostafaMaroof) listens once; the small layer says ضَ with probability 0.65 — above 0.3, so “passed”. It does not count sounds, so the held vowel does not matter.

Recording 3 of “dad + fatha” from the open letter set. Every value comes from the bot’s own code and from the new check.

More details

Why it is required

Telegram’s Bot Platform terms prohibit data collection for datasets and ML models, except with the user’s “individual, explicit, active and revocable consent”. A notice line is not enough; violating it can end the bot (Telegram Bot Platform Developer Terms of Service, §4–5).

What the learner must understand before pressing

  • It will be published — in the title and on the button
  • Anyone, anywhere can download it
  • A voice is not anonymous
  • Downloaded copies cannot be recalled
  • Consent can be withdrawn at any time
