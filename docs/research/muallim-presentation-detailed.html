Task 1 · How it works

Every system compares expected sounds with heard ones; most never decide what to tell the learner

Language model hides errors

Word recognisers pick the likeliest word: Whisper large-v3 gets 98.78% of words wrong on Quran audio once vowel signs count (arXiv:2507.13977).

Canonical bias

A model trained only on correct reading hears the rule: in 81 of 94 clips with an error it reported the expected sound (Quran Lab, its own model card).

No decision step

Models output sounds; whether to say “wrong”, “say it again” or nothing is left to the app.

Task 1 · The four designs

Every system finds errors in one of four ways; none is proven on adult beginners

A · Transcribe, then compare

Write down every sound, align with the expected. Expected b-a, heard t-a → substitution.

Muaalem, HisnLabs, IQRA, QVoice · Muallim today. Proven: on expert reciters and errors made on purpose, not on learners

B · Verify each expected sound

Score “is it b, or the t learners say instead?”. Flag when low.

HAFSS, Quran Lab, Azure. Proven: the only ones with numbers on learners — 32–46% of errors caught (makers’ own)

C · Pick from a closed list

For one letter with a vowel there are 84 possible answers: which one was read?

Children’s letter tools; our checker is built this way. Proven: 85% agreement with people (Black 2011); ours 88% on other students

D · Compare with a teacher’s voice

Align learner and teacher recordings in time; measure the distance.

Maqam, TajweedMate. Proven: nothing published; says “how far”, not “which sound”

Muallim today = A, with no numbers on learners. Plan: C for single letters, B for words (slide 30).

Task 1 · Inside other systems

Four systems opened up: the best ones decide carefully, not hear better

HAFSS · 2006

RDI and Cairo University; for non-native learners

  • · Unit = consonant + vowel, as a teacher counts, not phonemes
  • · 663 rules of typical errors build a net: correct reading + likely wrong ones
  • · The recogniser may only choose inside that net
  • · Confidence → 4 answers: stay silent · ask to repeat · “an error” · name it
  • · 507 phrases: 62.4% of errors named, 22.4% asked again, 14.9% missed

Quran Lab · TajweedFormer V4 · 2026

non-profit lab; the best documented today

  • · 65 million parameters, runs on a phone CPU, live
  • · 38 sounds + a “foreign sound” token; separate outputs for length and doubling
  • · No language model, on purpose: it must not “fix” the learner
  • · Gives no verdict; the app sets thresholds per error type
  • · Learners: 32–46% of errors caught (its own card)

HisnLabs · tajweed-v2 · 2026

open, Apache-2.0

  • · 20.6 million parameters (23 MB): 4 s of audio in 28 ms
  • · A small copy of Muaalem, retrained with learner recordings
  • · Aligns heard with expected; rules name the error
  • · Two thresholds: 0.3 “remark”, 0.7 “clear mistake”
  • · Length judged against the reader’s own pace

Tarteel

largest app; paid, ≈$99 a year

  • · Recognises words, compares them with the verse
  • · Finds missed, wrong, extra words
  • · Independent review: does not see vowel-sign errors
  • · Built for memorisation, not for learning letters
  • · Method and accuracy on learners not published

From the papers and model cards (SOLUTIONS_ARABIC §6; Phase 1 §3.8). HAFSS: Abdou et al., Interspeech 2006. Quran Lab and HisnLabs figures are the makers’ own.

Even the best system in the world misses most real learner errors

Task 2 · The test data

8,904 recordings of other people’s students; the right answer is what they were asked to read

The dataset

  • · “Pelafalan Huruf Hijaiyah”, open on Hugging Face (AFL-3.0)
  • · Students of an Indonesian Islamic school; age and level not stated
  • · 28 letters × 3 vowels = 84 items, exactly the first stage of our book
  • · 8,904 clips of 0.5–2.4 s; 48 students (grouped by first name)
  • · Step 1: a sample of 840 = 84 items × 10 students

What it cannot tell

  • · No teacher checked the clips: “right” = the item asked for
  • · Not our learners: Indonesian pupils, not Russian-speaking adults
  • · No marked errors, so “wrong” readings were simulated from other letters
  • · Why not our own: no recordings collected under public consent yet
  • · The bot’s 84 library recordings say the vowel’s name aloud and are partly mislabelled — unusable as a test (22 of 84 matched)

So the tests answer: does a system recognise which letter and vowel a learner meant? Not yet: does it catch our learners’ real errors.

Task 2 · The bot against the checker

Today the bot would say “repeat” in 86 of 100 recordings; only a teacher can say who is right

Same 840 recordings; 86 of 100 = 725 of 840. The two differ in the listening model and in the decision, and the checker was trained on other students of this same set. “Bot today”: the bot’s own code — its references, its comparison and its 80-point pass mark — applied to its model’s output. The commonest reason is a held vowel: the student says the letter slowly and the bot calls it too long. Who is right in each case only a teacher can say; that is why our own labelled recordings come next.

Task 1 · Conclusion

Letters: choose from 84 items; words: Muaalem plus a check of each sound; word recognisers and voice LLMs are not judges

Stage I · lessons of single letters and syllables

Verify from a closed list

Classifier over the 84 items on top of a phoneme model; trained on our learners’ labelled voices. On other people’s students: 88% vs 47%.

Stage II · lessons of words and lines

Phoneme model + per-sound check

Keep Muaalem, add scoring of each expected sound against its usual substitutes, thresholds per error type — the method of Quran Lab and HisnLabs.

Both stages

A decision policy

Accept · say it again · name the error. HAFSS did this in 2006. Ours, answering only when sure (3 clips in 4), was right in 95.8% of them; the rest get “say it again”.

Not as the judge: word recognisers

Whisper, Tarteel-style: cannot see vowel errors.

Not as the judge: voice LLMs

15–17% sound errors without training (papers, Phase 1), slow, no learner evidence.

Not: commercial APIs

No Arabic sound labels; learner voices leave our server.

Muallim inside · Architecture

The speech model sits behind its own socket: it can be replaced without touching the bot

Layers checked automatically on every change: the rules (domain) know nothing about Telegram or the database; the speech model sits behind a port, so another one can be plugged in without touching the rest. Every attempt keeps the reference, the model’s raw answer, versions and thresholds.

Task 3 · Labels and size

Two teachers label every test recording without seeing the bot’s answer

Error labels

Two sets, kept apart

System: 10 kinds — other letter, other vowel, vowel too long / short, missing vowel, no doubling, missing or extra sound…
Teacher: letter, vowel, length, missing, extra, shadda, sukun; + emphasis, nasal, qalqala, hesitation, wrong stop.
New: a symbol for a sound that is not Arabic (a Russian substitute).

Quality of labels

Measured, not assumed

At least 2 teachers per test recording, blind to the bot · agreement reported per release (Krippendorff’s α, Cohen’s κ) · disagreements kept · each teacher described: tajwid training, first language. For scale: QuranMB 3 linguists, speechocean762 5 experts, qdat_bench 1.

Size and split

20–40 learners for a first release

Split by learner: ≈70% train, ≈30% test, so 6–12 test speakers · test list frozen before any training · every figure with an interval over speakers · invalid audio kept with a reason (in a similar set a quarter of recordings were unusable).

Why the bot cannot export a public dataset today — 5 gaps

1 no record of consent to public release · 2 the learner code comes from an internal id and must not be published · 3 no train / test assignment · 4 no dataset card, licence, checksums · 5 no symbol for a non-Arabic sound. Everything else (reference, model answer, versions, teacher labels, export) is already stored.

Task 4 · Public consent

“Continue” means “I agree to publication”

Muallim

Your recordings will become part of an open dataset

If you press “Continue”, your voice messages are published on the internet. Any researcher in any country can download them. Your name, Telegram account and phone are not published.

Your voice is you: someone may recognise it. You can withdraw at any time; copies already downloaded cannot be taken back. Only adults’ recordings are published.

Continue — I agree to publication

More details

Why it is required

Telegram’s Bot Platform terms prohibit data collection for datasets and ML models, except with the user’s “individual, explicit, active and revocable consent”. A notice line is not enough; violating it can end the bot (Telegram Bot Platform Developer Terms of Service, §4–5).

What the learner must understand before pressing

  • It will be published — in the title and on the button
  • Anyone, anywhere can download it
  • A voice is not anonymous
  • Downloaded copies cannot be recalled
  • Saying no is fine: the bot works the same

Task 4 · Behind the button

Four states, and what happens on withdrawal

Withdrawal

  • Recording stops at once
  • Audio and labels deleted
  • Next release omits the speaker
  • Dataset terms oblige users to delete it
  • Downloaded copies cannot be recalled — said before pressing

Already collected recordings cannot be published: the present consent says “only researchers”; publication needs a new question.

Rules in code: no audio written before consent · publishable only if consent was valid when recorded · a changed text asks again · under 18 or no age: never published · every release rebuilt from current consents. The present consent says “only researchers”, so nothing recorded under it can be published without asking again.
