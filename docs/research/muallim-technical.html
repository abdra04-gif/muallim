How a bot can check a beginner’s Arabic reading

Four questions — four answers: models, experiments, dataset, consent

Telegram trainer for al-Muallim ath-Thani · adults from zero · Abdirakhim Ismailov · 18 November 2026

Four questions

The questions and the short answers

1

Which models and solutions can transcribe a beginner’s reading?

No ready model is made for adult beginners. The right base: models that write down sounds, not words.

2

We ran 5 models and built 4 ways of deciding. Best: choose from the 84 syllables — 9 of 10 instead of 5 of 10.

3

Audio files + one table: one line per attempt, with what should be read, what the bot heard and what teachers heard.

4

One screen before the first recording; the button itself says “I agree to publication”.

Before we start

Nine words that appear on the slides

Model

a program trained on thousands of hours of speech; it turns a voice into letters or sounds

Phoneme (sound)

one sound of speech: “b”, “a”, “s”

Vowel sign (haraka)

a small mark over or under a letter that gives its vowel: بَ “ba”, بِ “bi”, بُ “bu”

Syllable

a letter with a vowel sign; in the first stage of the book there are 28 × 3 = 84 of them

Probability

how sure the model is, from 0 (surely not) to 1 (surely yes)

Fingerprint

3,072 numbers the model computes inside while listening; similar sounds give similar numbers

Classifier

a small program that looks at the fingerprint and says which of the 84 syllables it is

Threshold

the minimum certainty needed to say “passed”

List-of-84 check

our proposal for letters: the model listens once, a classifier picks which of the 84 syllables was read

Question 1 · two kinds of models

To check a beginner, the model must write down sounds, not guess words

In simple words: an editor corrects mistakes — a teacher must not. So the base for the bot is a “stenographer” model. (Technically: CTC models without a language model.)

Question 1 · inside a sound model

A sound model cuts the voice into 20-ms frames and names a sound for each

In simple words: the model first learned on thousands of hours of speech without any text (“self-supervised”), then on recordings with known sounds. All models we ran except Whisper and Tarteel are built like this; they differ in size and in what they were taught on.

Question 1 · speed and size

Sound models answer in a fifth of a second; Whisper is several times slower

In simple words: a learner waits less than a second — speed is not a problem. Whisper is slow because it writes words one by one. The list-of-84 check adds a 0.5-MB file and almost no time.

Only the model call, without decoding the audio; MacBook Air M1; the device (CPU or GPU) differed between models — Phase 3, tables 2.3 and 3.1.

Question 1 · what the research found

Four main findings

Nothing for adult beginners

We read 130+ sources: 40+ models, 25 datasets, 36 products. All are for reciters, children or English learners.

Editors hide errors

Whisper gets 99 Quran words of 100 wrong once vowel signs count.

Even the best catches under half

The best documented system finds 32–46 of 100 real learner errors (its makers’ figures).

For letters: check, don’t write down

Children’s letter tools check the expected sound; a 2025 study did the same for Arabic letters (≈65%).

In simple words: each cell is one letter with fatha (“a”), kasra (“i”) and damma (“u”). The bot always shows one of these 84 cards — so it knows in advance which syllable should sound, and the answer is always one of 84.

Question 2 · how it will work in the bot

The bot knows the card, so it only asks: is this the syllable on the card?

Where it is used

Only in lessons of single letters and syllables (stage I). For words and lines there are too many possible answers — there the bot checks each sound (Muaalem).

What must be done

Record our learners reading the 84 syllables, have teachers label them, train the classifier on these voices (minutes) and plug it in next to Muaalem.

Question 2 · four ways to decide

The model that listens is the same — we changed only how the answer is chosen

1 · Write down and compare

The model writes the sounds it heard; we compare them with the asked syllable.

like a dictation — this is how the bot works now

47%

2 · Only real letters allowed

The same, but the answer must be one of the 28 letters and 3 vowels.

like a test with a hint: “it is one of these letters”

55%

3 · Compare with samples

Compare the recording with the average sound of each syllable from other students.

like holding a photo next to reference photos

70%

4 · Trained choice of 1 of 84

A small program has heard many examples of each syllable and picks the most likely one.

like an experienced teacher who has heard each syllable many times

88%

Number = how many recordings of 100 were recognised, on all 8,904. Way 1 is how the bot decides today; way 4 is what we propose for letters. Next two slides: what the “fingerprint” and the “small program” are.

Question 2 · what a “fingerprint” is

While listening, the model turns a sound into a set of numbers — like a fingerprint

In simple words: the same syllable from different people gives similar numbers; a different syllable gives different ones. So by the numbers you can tell which syllable was read. (Picture is schematic: 28 of the 3,072 numbers.)

Question 2 · what the “small program” is

The classifier gives each of the 84 syllables a score and turns the scores into probabilities

How it learns

We show it thousands of fingerprints with the right answer. It adjusts its weights until the right syllable gets the highest score. Takes minutes on a laptop.

Why the name “logistic regression”

It is the name of this simplest kind of classifier: a weighted sum of numbers turned into probabilities. No big neural network is trained.

Probabilities in the picture: ضَ = 0.65 is real (our example recording); the other values are illustrative.

Question 2 · a fair test

The program was always checked on students it had never heard

In simple words: students are split into 5 groups. The program learns on 4 groups and is checked on the 5th, then again with another group, 5 times. Like an exam with questions the student has not seen — otherwise it would just remember voices.

Question 2 · result

Choosing from the 84 syllables recognises 9 recordings of 10

In simple words: the gain came not from a better model but from a better way to decide. The result is stable: across students from 7 to 10 of 10.

Exact: 88.0% (95% interval 86.8–89.1) vs 47.2% for way 1 with the same model; 8,904 recordings, 48 students.

Question 2 · where it is still wrong

About 1 look-alike letter in 20 slips through; the weakest letter is ظ

How we tested errors

We took a recording of plain س and asked: “is this ص?” A good check must say “no”. Our check wrongly said “yes” about 5 times in 100.

5 of 100

Ask again when unsure

If the check stays silent on the quarter of recordings it is least sure about, it is right on 96 of 100 of the rest. The price: one learner in four repeats once more.

96 of 100

Hardest letters: ظ (only 59 of 100 correct readings passed) and the pair ث/س (up to 16 of 100 swaps let through). These sounds do not exist in Russian either.

Question 2 · choosing the threshold

A stricter threshold lets fewer errors through but rejects more correct readings

In simple words: the threshold is a balance between “scold for nothing” and “miss an error”. We took the middle, 0.3. For beginners scolding for nothing is worse (it discourages), so on our data we may choose a softer one.

Question 2 · in the bot

In the bot the check gives one of three answers

In simple words: “sure” means the probability of the asked syllable is at least 0.3; “not sure” — from 0.1 to 0.3; below 0.1 — the check names what it heard instead. On the 840 test recordings: passed 739, “say it again” 41, error 60.

Why 0.3: we tried 0.1, 0.3 and 0.5. Lower passes more correct readings but more errors too; 0.3 is the middle. On our own recordings we will choose again.

In simple words: the student most likely just held the vowel, as people do when saying one letter. The bot counts every sound, so an extra sound lowers its score; the list-of-84 check looks at the syllable as a whole.

Question 2 · the bot today

On the same 840 recordings the bot passes 14 of 100; the list-of-84 check 88 of 100

How the bot scores

It puts the heard sounds under the expected ones and counts matches: 2 matched of 2 expected + 4 heard → 2 × 2 / 6 = 67. 80 and more = “passed”.

Two problems found

1) A held vowel counts as an error — in 204 recordings it is the only reason for “repeat”. 2) For a single letter one allowed reading has no vowel at all, so a letter read without a vowel can pass (7 of 115).

No teacher checked these recordings, so who is right in each case is not known yet. Fixing both problems is a methodology decision of the author.

Question 2 · limits

What these tests do not show yet

  • The recordings are of Indonesian pupils, not our Russian-speaking adults.
  • No teacher checked them: “right” = the syllable that was asked.
  • Errors were simulated by swapping letters, not taken from real mistakes.
  • Only single syllables were tested; words and lines — not yet.
Next test: the same scripts on our own learners’ recordings, labelled by two teachers.

Question 3 · labelling rules

Five rules that make the labels trustworthy

Who labelstwo teachers for each test recording, each without seeing the bot’s answer
When it countsonly if both wrote the same; otherwise marked “disagreed” and both kept
How good the labels arewe measure how often teachers agree beyond chance (a number from 0 to 1)
Training and testsplit by learner: one person is either in training or in test, never both
How many20–40 learners × 84 syllables ≈ 1,700–3,400 recordings for the first release

Not published: name, Telegram account, exact dates. Published about a learner: age band, gender, first language. The bot already stores almost all of this; 5 small things are missing.

Question 4 · consent

One screen before the first recording — the button itself says what happens

4

How to make it clear that “Continue” = consent to a public dataset?

Your recordings will become part of an open dataset

Your voice messages will be published on the internet. Name, Telegram and phone — no. Someone may recognise your voice. You can withdraw at any time; copies already downloaded cannot be recalled.

Continue — I agree to publication

Why it cannot be misread

  • the button says “agree to publication”, not just “next”
  • the main fact is in the title
  • before consent nothing is recorded
  • the text changes — we ask again

Proposed, not yet in the bot. Telegram’s rules allow datasets only with explicit consent of this kind. Legal details — later.

Question 4 · what happens after the button

Four states: no consent, agreed, published, withdrawn

In simple words: each recording remembers which consent text was in force. If someone withdraws, their audio and labels are deleted and the next release goes out without them. The next release is always rebuilt from current consents.
