Recognising beginners' Arabic reading: what exists, what we built, what we need

17 November 2026 · Telegram reading trainer for al-Muallim ath-Thani, for Russian-speaking adults who start from zero

In short

  • No ready-made model is proven for our learners. Nothing published covers adults who start Arabic reading from zero, or Russian- and Kazakh-speaking learners.
  • We built and tested the most promising design. For single letters with a vowel sign, a checker that picks from the 84 possible answers was right in 88 % of recordings of students it had never heard. Free transcription by the same model was right in 47 %.
  • The dataset format is ready as a proposal: one record per attempt, original audio, three transcription layers, split by learner.
  • Consent must be explicit. Telegram's rules require it. A one-screen draft is ready; pressing "Continue" means "I agree to publication".
  • The decisive next step is our own recordings, labelled by a teacher. Every number below is measured on other people's recordings.

1. What we built and tested

Step 1 — ready-made systems

Five systems listened to the same 840 public recordings: 28 letters × 3 vowel signs × 10 students. The students were Indonesian school pupils, recorded for an open dataset.

Step 2 — our own checkers

Step 1 confirmed what the literature says: free transcription fails on single letters. So we built the alternative the research points to. All four checkers use the same listening model, the best one from step 1. Only the way of deciding differs.

Checker How it decides
Free transcription Writes down what it hears, then we read the first letter and vowel
Constrained choice May only choose among the 28 letters and 3 vowels
Template comparison Compares the recording with other students' average recording of each of the 84 items
Trained classifier A small layer trained to pick one of the 84 items

They were tested on all 8,904 recordings. Each recording was judged by a checker trained on other students only.

The bot does not need to know what was read. It knows what should be read and asks one question: was it read? That is the test that matters.

"Not sure" helps. If the classifier answers only when it is confident, it answers three recordings out of four and is right in 95.8 % of them. On the rest the bot can ask the learner to say it again.

What this does not show. The labels say what each student was asked to read, not what a teacher heard. Substitutions were simulated from other students' recordings. The students are not ours. The classifier must be retrained and checked on our learners' recordings before the bot uses it.

On the bot's own recordings. The project's library recordings of letters could not serve as a check: each contains the spoken name of the vowel as well as the syllable, and some are known to be mislabelled. A script is ready to check any new voice messages as soon as they exist.

Telegram's developer terms forbid collecting data for datasets unless the user gives "individual, explicit, active and revocable consent". A notice line is not enough.

1. Summary

  1. The task is not ordinary speech recognition. A Muallim learner reads a known text (a letter with a vowel sign, a syllable, a word). The system must report what sounds were actually produced, including wrong ones. Ordinary ASR is built to output the most likely word, so it tends to hide exactly the errors we need to see. The right problem class is mispronunciation detection and diagnosis (MDD) over a phoneme-level recogniser.
  2. No available system has been evaluated on our population. We found no model, open or commercial, with published results on adult beginners who start from zero, and none on Russian-speaking learners. The organisers of the main Arabic benchmark (IQRA 2026) state that collecting L2 learner data is future work.
  3. Phoneme-level CTC models built on self-supervised encoders are the strongest evidence-backed family. They took the top places at IQRA 2026 (best F1 0.72). Text ASR, audio LLMs and the one commercial pronunciation API all score lower where a comparison exists.
  4. Small amounts of real error data matter more than large synthetic sets. At IQRA 2026, 1,333 real mispronounced utterances helped the top systems more than 55,400 synthetic ones. This supports collecting our own dataset.
  5. Isolated letters are a known weak point. On a corpus of single vowelled letters (the Horouf corpus), an off-the-shelf Arabic wav2vec 2.0 model reached 35–37 % accuracy; a small classifier on its embeddings reached about 65 %. Stage I of Muallim is exactly this material.
  6. A new model family exists that the project did not know: Quran Lab (2026). Its newest model, TajweedFormer V4 (November 2026), is the only one found with published numbers on real learners. At a low false-alarm rate it catches 46 % of consonant errors of non-native speakers (L2-KSU) and 32 % of vowel errors corrected by teachers. So the best documented system still misses more than half of real learner errors.
  7. "Canonical bias" is documented by the model authors themselves. A model trained on correct recitation outputs what the rule says, not what was said: one model reported the expected sound in 81 of 94 clips where annotators heard something else. This is the central technical risk for any model we choose.
  8. Licences now matter for the choice. Quran Lab models forbid charging for any feature they power. The thesis has a business part, so this needs the author's decision before such a model enters the product.
  9. There is a route that needs no recogniser at all: compare the learner's audio directly with a teacher's recording of the same item. In 2026 work on English and Japanese this reached human-level agreement for sentence-level scoring. The bot already holds human reference recordings, so this can be tested cheaply.
  10. Recommended design to test in Phase 3: not one model but three mechanisms on the same audio — a phoneme model with per-sound verification, a closed-set classifier for single letters, and direct comparison with reference recordings (sections 3.10 and 8).

2. What the task requires

Requirement Why it matters for Muallim Consequence
Verbatim phonetic output The error is the signal Models with a strong language model "repair" errors
Short vowels and gemination in the output Learners read vowelled text; fatha/kasra/damma confusions are core errors Undiacritized ASR cannot see vowel errors at all
Very short audio (one letter, one syllable) Stage I = 28 letters with vowel signs Models trained on sentences behave unpredictably on 1-second clips
Sounds outside the Arabic inventory A Russian speaker may substitute a Russian sound Arabic-only phoneme sets force every sound into an Arabic label
Slow, hesitant, restarted speech Normal for beginners Long pauses and restarts cause deletions and hallucinations
Trainable on little data The pilot will give thousands of short attempts, not hours Prefer models with a public fine-tuning path
Runs on our server, open licence Learner voices are personal data; public dataset planned Commercial APIs are comparison points, not the product core

Evidence that text ASR hides learner errors:

  • In Dutch primary-school reading, Whisper's main failure was omitting incorrect attempts (207 cases), not mis-transcribing them; miscue-detection F1 was 0.52. [S] Gao et al., Interspeech 2024, arXiv:2406.07060.
  • Post-hoc comparison of ASR output with the target text "performs poorly when ASR inaccurately transcribes verbatim speech"; prompting Whisper with the target text improved results on children's read-aloud speech. [S] Smith et al., Interspeech 2025, arXiv:2505.23627.
  • Whisper large-v3 scores 98.78 % WER on Quran audio when diacritics are counted, because it does not output them. [P] Grigoryan et al., arXiv:2507.13977, Table V.

Findings:

  • Quran Lab is the most relevant new source. It is described on its own site as a non-profit lab. It publishes a benchmark with a ranking metric built for our problem ("checker score": correct readings must be left alone and the wrong word must be found), and its model cards report failures openly.
  • Three independent authors recommend the same mechanism: do not read errors off the decoded text; instead score the expected sound against alternatives at each position (TajweedFormer reference method, MostafaMaroof card, Muno459's likelihood signal).
  • All of these models are built for Quran text. Whether their text-to-sound tools accept a single vowelled letter or a non-Quran word is not documented (quran-g2p examples use verse references only). The bot already solves this for Muaalem; for any other model it must be checked.

What I noticed across the sources

  1. The numbers that look best say the least about us. Error rates below 2 % are all measured on professional reciters. On amateurs, phone audio, children or unseen reciters the same models show 4–9 %. The only results on real learner errors are recall figures of 32–46 %.
  2. Almost every figure is self-reported. Only the IQRA leaderboard is run by a party other than the model's author. Quran Lab's learner sets are private, so its key numbers cannot be reproduced.
  3. Sources contradict themselves. The IQRA paper gives two values for the 2025 best score; the Horouf paper gives 35 % and 37 %; the NVIDIA paper's conclusion disagrees with its own table; the Muaalem paper has two sets of error rates across versions. None of this changes the conclusions, but no single figure should be quoted without the table it came from.
  4. Model cards that admit failure are the most useful. The statements "outputs the rule, not what was said" and "not validated for mispronunciation detection" came from authors, not critics.
  5. Nobody evaluates what Muallim teaches first. Published results concern verses and sentences. The two letter-level results (35–37 % and 62 %) are both poor.
  6. Licences split the field in two. Permissive: Muaalem, tajweed-v2, NVIDIA, Whisper, OmniASR, ZIPA. Restricted: Quran Lab (no paid features), IqraEval baseline encoder, MMS, SeamlessM4T (non-commercial).
  7. The project's present approach is the one the field converged on: a phoneme model without a language model, compared with a reference. The research did not find a different approach that is clearly better; it found ways to make this one more reliable.

Proposals

Strengthen

  • Replace reading errors off the decoded text with scoring each expected sound against alternatives, with a separate threshold per error class. Three independent authors recommend it (3.8).
  • Report results the way the Quran Lab benchmark does: correct readings left alone and wrong ones found, in one figure. It makes false alarms visible, which matter most for beginners' motivation.
  • Keep the "not sure" outcome already planned in ADR-0051. The 32–46 % recall of the best system shows that a confident verdict on every sound is not achievable now.

Add

  • A Stage I path that does not transcribe: a closed-set classifier or comparison with the teacher's recording (3.10, 8).
  • A universal phone recogniser as a second opinion for sounds that are not Arabic.
  • Hisnlabs/tajweed-v2 as a fallback when server load matters.
  • Contact with Quran Lab, the only group holding learner-error test sets.

Remove or deprioritise

  • General ASR and audio LLMs as detectors — keep only as controls.
  • Commercial pronunciation APIs — no Arabic sound labels, weaker agreement with experts, data leaves the server.
  • Chasing leaderboard word-error rankings: they measure a different task.

Do not claim in the thesis

  • That any model "works for beginners": no published evidence exists.
  • That Muallim's model choice was validated: the Phase 3 test was preliminary and did not use our learners.

How to compare them

Borrow the Quran Lab ranking idea: on a balanced set of correct and incorrect attempts, count the share that gets exactly the right verdict. A system that never flags anything scores 50 %. This punishes both missed errors and false alarms, and it applies equally to a phoneme model, a classifier and a reference comparison.

  2. L2-KSU can support our research as an external test, not as training data. It has 40 non-native speakers with phone-level labels, but they are African students already studying Arabic, reading ten full sentences. Its licence forbids derivative works. It is the best available check that a model catches real non-native consonant errors.
  3. Russian- and Kazakh-speaking learners are not absent from existing data. The RetaSy dataset (about 7,000 recordings, 1,287 participants) lists Russia, Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan among its countries. It has only a verse-level "correct / has mistakes" label, and only 1,166 recordings are labelled. Its authors overlap with the authors of sources the project attributes to the supervisor's group, so fuller access may be possible.
  4. A public letter-level dataset from non-Arab learners exists. Reinjin/Pelafalan_Huruf_Hijaiyah: 8,904 clips, 84 classes (28 letters × 3 vowel signs), Indonesian boarding-school students, open licence. It is the same material as Stage I of Muallim. It has no error labels and almost no documentation.
  5. The field's benchmarks are small. The main Arabic error test set has 1,643 utterances; the main learner-recitation benchmark has 159 recordings. A Muallim dataset of 20–40 learners and a few thousand attempts would be of comparable size.
  6. Errors in the main benchmarks are not learner errors. IQRA's test sets use native speakers who mispronounce on instruction, or synthetic speech.
  7. Muallim's contribution would be the first open dataset that follows the same beginners through a graded textbook, from single letters to words, with teacher-verified sound-level error labels (section 7).

Aspect Finding

Can it support our research?

  • Yes, as an external test set. It is the only obtainable corpus with sound-level labels on real non-native adult speech, and two published results exist to compare against.
  • No, as training data for our learners. Different first languages, different level, sentences instead of letters, and ten prompts only.
  • Legal caution. "No derivatives" may cover a model fine-tuned on the data and forbids sharing a modified copy. Use for evaluation is the safe reading; training on it needs the author's decision.

Observations for Phase 4:

  • The expected-vs-produced scheme is the standard and the project's export already follows it. [R]
  • RetaSy shows that "invalid audio" categories are needed in crowd-collected data: 25 % of its labelled recordings were empty, the wrong verse, several verses, or incomplete.
  • Agreement is rarely reported. RetaSy reports α = 0.63 with three judges; qdat_bench has one annotator; most Hugging Face sets report nothing.
  • No dataset records the same learner over time, and none records the system's answer next to the teacher's.

What I noticed

  1. Documentation is the weakest part of this field. Of eleven Arabic learner or error datasets, three have an empty description page and four give no licence. A well-documented dataset of modest size would stand out.
  2. Agreement between annotators is almost never reported. Only RetaSy gives a figure. qdat_bench, behind many published numbers, has a single annotator.
  3. "Non-native" hides very different people. L2-KSU's institute students, RetaSy's worshippers reciting from memory and Indonesian boarding-school pupils are all "non-native", and none is an adult opening an Arabic primer for the first time.
  4. The standard benchmark does not contain learner errors at all, and its organisers say so.
  5. Every useful learner set is restricted in some way: no derivatives (L2-KSU), no licence (RetaSy), non-commercial (IqraEval), on request (Horouf), private (Quran Lab).
  6. Consent is not described anywhere. No dataset page or paper read in this phase explains what participants agreed to. For RetaSy this is notable, since it contains voices of identifiable countries and ages.
  7. Counts do not always add up. L2-KSU: 4,094 files, 818 rows. RetaSy: "about 7,000" in the paper, 6,828 on the hub, 1,166 labelled, 1,427 "with annotations".
  8. Women and children are scarce in correct-recitation data but not in learner data (qdat_bench is 75 % women, RetaSy 47 %). Models trained on the former are tested on the latter.

Proposals for Muallim's dataset

Strengthen

  • Make documentation and agreement reporting the dataset's selling point: full datasheet, annotator description, α per error type. This is cheap and rare (observations 1–2).
  • Use a permissive licence if consent allows. speechocean762's free licence is a main reason it is used everywhere.
  • Describe the consent procedure in the dataset card. No comparable dataset does (observation 6).
  • Balance recruitment by first language and gender as far as the pilot allows, following L2-ARCTIC.

Add

  • A small native or expert control group reading the same items, as L2-KSU has. It shows what "correct" sounds like on our material and in our recording conditions, and it gives reference recordings.
  • Invalid-recording categories kept in the data (RetaSy found 25 % invalid).
  • Optionally, a teacher's overall score per attempt on a short scale, as in speechocean762. It is fast to give and allows comparison with scoring systems. This is a methodology decision for the author.
  • A statement of exact counts at every stage — recorded, consented, labelled, agreed — to avoid the mismatches seen above.

Remove from the plan or avoid

  • Do not use staged errors or synthetic speech in the test set. They may be used for training only, and labelled as such.
  • Do not rely on a single annotator for any published figure.
  • Do not train on L2-KSU while its licence forbids derivatives, unless the author and supervisor decide otherwise.
  • Do not claim "first dataset of non-Arab learners": RetaSy, L2-KSU, L2AraSpeech and the Indonesian letter set exist. The defensible claim is in section 7.

Limits to state honestly in the thesis:

  • The claim "no Russian or Kazakh learner data exists" would be false; RetaSy contains such speakers. The accurate claim is about sound-level labels and beginner material.
  • A dataset from one textbook and one or two first languages will not generalise to other learner groups.
  • The number of speakers, not the number of recordings, limits what can be concluded.

1. Summary

  1. No system recognises a single vowelled letter reliably. The best one reports the right letter and the right vowel in 50.0 % of clips.
  2. The bot's present model (Muaalem) is not the best on this material: 39.4 % against 50.0 % for a smaller Arabic phoneme model.
  3. Muaalem writes a long vowel in 411 of 840 clips although every clip is one letter with a short vowel. A learner who pronounces a letter slowly would be told the vowel is too long.
  4. The confusions are phonetically close pairs — emphatic and plain, voiced and voiceless, ث and س. These are the pairs a beginner confuses too, so the models are weakest where the bot most needs them.
  5. Text recognisers turn sounds into words. Whisper wrote a vowel sign in 3 of 840 clips. The Quran-trained Whisper often answered with a real word instead of the letter.
  6. Speed is not a constraint for phoneme models: about 0.2 seconds per clip on a laptop.
  7. What this test cannot say: how well any model judges our learners, and how often a mismatch is the student's error rather than the model's. See section 7.
  8. Built and tested (step 2): a small classifier over the 84 letter–vowel answers is right in 88.0 % of clips from students it never heard, against 47.2 % for free transcription by the same model. Used as the bot would use it — "was the expected item read?" — it accepts 90.5 % of the readings and wrongly accepts 5.2 % of readings where a confusable letter was said instead. Free transcription accepts only 47.2 % of the readings. Section 11.

2.4 Measure

For each clip the script takes the first consonant in the system's output and the first vowel after it, and compares them with the class.

  • Letter right — the first consonant is the expected letter.
  • Vowel right — the first vowel is the expected one (a, i or u; long and short count the same).
  • Both right — both conditions.
  • No letter found — the output is empty or has no consonant.

WER, CER and PER were not computed. They need a transcription of what was actually said, and this dataset has only the intended class. With one letter per clip, "both right" is the closest meaningful measure.

For the universal phone recogniser the four emphatic letters (ص ض ط ظ) are excluded from the letter score: its inventory has no symbols for them (section 4.4).

4. Observations

4.1 Muaalem (the bot's model)

  • Vowel a is right in 94.6 % of clips, but i and u in about 60 %. When the vowel is wrong, the model most often wrote a: 60 of 280 clips for i and 74 of 280 for u.
  • In 411 of 840 clips the output contains a long vowel (for example قِۦۦ, ءَاا), although the class is a short one. Students hold the vowel when they say a letter alone; the model, trained on recitation, reads that as a prolongation.
  • 22 clips gave an empty output.
  • Its letter score (50.4 %) is below the smaller maroof model (65.7 %). The gap is largest on ب, ه, ع, ك, ز, ش, ل.

4.2 The Arabic phoneme model maroof

  • Best overall and the fewest empty answers (1.0 %).
  • Weak on ث (13.3 %) and on all four emphatic letters (26.7–40.0 %).

4.3 What the errors have in common

Across the three phone-level systems the same pairs recur: د→ت, ض→د, ث→س, ظ→ز or ذ, ص→س or ش. In each pair the two sounds differ by one feature (voicing, emphasis, or place of the tongue). The dataset cannot tell us which side erred: an Indonesian student may really have said س for ث. Both readings lead to the same conclusion for the bot — on these pairs a verdict from free transcription cannot be trusted.

4.4 The universal phone recogniser

  • It never reported ء, ح or ع: glottal and pharyngeal sounds are dropped or written as h.
  • For the emphatic letters it writes the plain counterpart: ص → s ×19 of 30, ض → d ×21, ط → t ×11, ظ → z ×17.
  • So it cannot check the sounds that are specific to Arabic. It remains usable only as a second opinion on sounds that exist in many languages.

4.5 Text recognisers

  • whisper: a vowel sign appears in 3 of 840 outputs. Frequent answers are words — «نعم» ×24, «لا» ×20 — and in 265 clips the output ran to ten or more characters, typically one letter repeated up to the length limit («اووووووووووو»).
  • tarteel: vowel signs in 446 of 840 outputs. Frequent answers are a bare letter (س ×65, ف ×50) or a real word: «ذِي» ×48, «فِي» ×34, «ذُو» ×32, «طه» ×19.
  • This matches Phase 1: recognisers built to output words replace an isolated sound by the nearest word.

7. Limitations

  1. Not our learners. Indonesian school students, not Russian-speaking adults; their first language has a different sound system.
  2. No ground truth for errors. A mismatch is either a model error or a student error. The scores are therefore a lower bound on model accuracy, and the test cannot measure error detection (missed errors, false alarms).
  3. A sample. 840 of 8,904 clips; 30 per letter. Per-letter figures are rough.
  4. Only the first consonant and vowel are scored. Extra sounds after them are ignored, which favours every system equally.
  5. Models as published. No adaptation, thresholds or per-sound confidence were used. The bot does not use Muaalem's transcription alone in the same way, so this is not a measurement of the bot.
  6. Speed across devices. Three systems ran on the Apple GPU and two on the CPU.
  7. Not tested: Quran Lab TajweedFormer V4 (licence and server access undecided), Hisnlabs/tajweed-v2, nrshoudi models, NVIDIA FastConformer (needs a toolkit that is not installed — a new dependency is the author's decision), Azure and Gemini (audio would leave our computer), L2-KSU (access not requested yet), reference comparison and the letter classifier.

The project holds 84 library recordings of single vowelled letters (curriculum/audio/library, SRC-144). They were run through the classifier with check_recordings.py. The classifier's best guess matched the label in 22 of 84; the vowel was most often heard as a (55 of 84).

11.7 What this shows

  1. Verification over the closed set is the right design for the first stage. On speakers it never heard, a simple classifier is right in 88 % of clips, against 47 % for free transcription by the same model. Nothing was tuned; the gain comes from the design.
  2. It answers the bot's real question far better. It accepts 90.5 % of correct readings instead of 47.2 %, while letting through 5.2 % of confusable substitutions.
  3. A "not sure" zone works as expected. Answering three clips out of four raises correctness to 95.8 %.
  4. Weak spots remain where they were: ظ (58.5 % of correct readings accepted) and the pair ث/س (11–16 % of substitutes accepted).

11.8 Limitations of step 2

  • The labels say what the student was asked to read, not what was said. The classifier learns how these students pronounce each item. If a group of students systematically says ز for ظ, the classifier learns to accept that. The substitute trials show that attempts at each letter of a pair are mostly kept apart, but whether any attempt was correct can only be known from teacher labels.
  • Substitutions are simulated from other students' attempts at the other letter, not from real errors.
  • Not our learners. Indonesian school students, not Russian-speaking adults. A classifier must be retrained on our own recordings.
  • One listening model. The same comparison with Muaalem or other encoders was not run.
  • Speakers were grouped by first name; two different students with the same first name are counted as one.
  • Step 1 chose "ten different students" per class by spacing file names; because of the name variants found here, a few of those ten may be the same student.

1. Summary

  1. The project already has most of this dataset. The bot stores every attempt with the reference, the model's answer, teacher labels and versions; an export already writes a manifest, benchmark-compatible tables and audio. This specification defines the public release built on that export and lists what is missing.
  2. Recommended format: one folder of audio files plus plain-text tables (JSON Lines and CSV), published on Hugging Face, which converts it to Parquet automatically. One canonical table, several derived ones.
  3. Unit of data: one attempt — one voice message in which one learner reads one textbook item.
  4. Three layers of transcription per attempt: what should be read (reference), what the model heard, and what teachers say was actually read. They are stored separately and never overwrite each other.
  5. Split by learner, never by recording. The test part is fixed before any training and its teacher labels are produced without seeing the model's answer.
  6. Seven gaps separate the current export from a publishable dataset (section 12). The most important: public release needs its own consent record, release-only speaker codes, and removal of religious-practice data.

Schema changes, the consent text, annotation rules and anything touching personal data are decisions for the author under project rules; here they are proposals.

Recommendation. Keep the current approach and formalise it:

  • canonical: attempts.jsonl (one attempt per line, nested annotations) + audio files;
  • derived from it by script: flat CSV tables and the two benchmark files;
  • published on Hugging Face in its audio-folder layout, so that the same files load there without conversion.

Reasons: it is what the project already produces; six of the nine datasets in section 3.1 are published on Hugging Face as audio plus tables; it is readable without code; and gated access on Hugging Face gives a place to require acceptance of usage conditions.

Checked against the Hugging Face documentation [P]: the metadata file must be named metadata.csv, metadata.jsonl or metadata.parquet; it must have a file_name column holding the relative path; it must sit in the same folder as the audio or a parent folder; OGG is a supported audio format; folders named after splits define the splits; the hub converts datasets to Parquet automatically up to 5 GB. The layout in section 4 follows these rules. NeMo's manifest layout is stated from general knowledge [?].

7. Text and transcription layers

Layer Field Who produces it Rule
Reference text reference_text The textbook, entered and checked by a person Fully vowelled Arabic exactly as shown to the learner. Unicode normalisation form NFC. No added or removed marks.
Reference sounds reference_phonemes The phonetiser from the reference text Stored with the version that produced it (inside model.version). Never edited by hand.
Model answer model.heard_phonemes The model Stored raw, with per-sound confidence.
Verified transcription annotations[].actual_phonemes, verified_phonemes Teachers Each annotator's sequence is kept. The verified field is filled only on agreement of at least two. Disagreements stay visible, they are not resolved silently.

Diacritics:

  • vowel signs, sukun, shadda and tanwin are always kept, in the reference text and in every sound sequence;
  • a derived column without diacritics may be generated for users of ordinary ASR tools, but it is not stored as a source field.

Sound inventory:

  • primary: the Quran Phonetic Script already used by the bot and by the Muaalem model and its relatives (Phase 1);
  • published mapping: a table from each symbol to IPA and, where one exists, to the 68-symbol set of the IQRA benchmark, in docs/phoneme-inventory.md. This lets other groups score models that use a different inventory;
  • sounds outside Arabic (a Russian substitute) need a symbol. Proposal: one reserved symbol for "non-Arabic sound" plus a free-text note; a finer inventory can be added after the first labelled batch shows which substitutes actually occur.

Time alignment: not annotated by hand. If automatic alignments are added later, they go into a separate field marked as automatic.

Set Values Where

9. Annotation guidelines

The project has an annotation guide with versions. For a public dataset it should state the following explicitly (most of it exists; items marked new do not):

Topic Rule
What is labelled What was actually pronounced, not whether the reading "deserves a pass"
Reference point The expected sound sequence; the annotator corrects it where the learner deviated
Uncertainty "Not sure" is allowed and recorded; unclear audio is audio_unusable, not a guess
Self-correction Marked separately; such attempts are excluded from headline figures
Hesitation, restart Marked as halting; not an articulation error
Number of annotators At least two per attempt in the test split
Blind labelling new as a recorded field: test-split labels are produced without seeing the model's answer
Agreement Reported per release: Krippendorff's α and Cohen's κ, per error type where numbers allow
Disagreement Kept in the data; a joint review may add a third label but does not delete the first two
Annotator description Published per code: qualification in tajwid, first language, hours of training — new
Non-Arabic sounds Reserved symbol plus note — new

10. Splits

Rule Specification
Unit of splitting The learner. All attempts of one learner are in one split.
Proportions About 70 % of learners for training and validation together, 30 % for test, as already decided in ADR-0051
Stratification By first language and by activity level, so that the test part is not all short-lived or all very active learners
Freezing The test list of speakers is fixed before any model is trained and published with its checksum
Validation Taken from the training learners by cross-validation over learners; a fixed validation list is published for convenience
Unlabelled Attempts with consent but without teacher labels are published separately for self-supervised use
Text overlap The same textbook items occur in every split. This is unavoidable and intended: the task is to judge new speakers reading known text. The card must say so.
New releases Learners never move between splits. New learners are assigned by the same rule.

With 20–40 learners the test part holds 6–12 speakers. Every reported figure needs a confidence interval computed over speakers.

14. Limitations

  • Not validated by running models (Phase 3 skipped). In particular, whether one reserved symbol for non-Arabic sounds is enough is unknown.
  • The example records show format only.
  • The Hugging Face layout rules were checked in its documentation (section 3.3); the layout itself has not been test-uploaded.
  • The L2-KSU paper could not be read, so its annotation procedure is known only from the dataset card.
  • Voice recordings cannot be made anonymous. Codes and coarse bands reduce the risk of identifying a learner; they do not remove it.
  • With few learners, a combination such as "female, 55+, Tatar" may identify one person. Rare combinations may need merging into other before release.
  • Whole-surah readings (a separate part of the bot's data) are outside this specification.

  1. Which licence will the dataset carry? Related datasets are mostly non-commercial; the choice affects who can use ours. (Phase 5.)

  3. Are learners under 18 excluded, or included with a guardian's consent?
  4. Is one dataset with gated access enough, or should the test labels be withheld and scored on request, as the Quran Lab benchmark does?
  5. Who are the annotators for the frozen test split, and how many attempts can they label?

16. Recommendations for Phase 5

  1. Design the public-release consent first; gaps 1–3 depend on it and nothing can be published without it.
  2. Decide the withdrawal procedure together with the release cycle: a withdrawn learner disappears from the next release, but copies already downloaded cannot be recalled — the consent text must say this plainly.
  3. Use gated access with usage conditions (no attempt to identify speakers, no voice cloning) as part of the licence design.
  4. Settle the rule for minors before the pilot, since the questionnaire already has an under-18 band.
  5. Reconcile with ADR-0085 (proposed: no consent screen, learner shown by name to the teacher). A public dataset requires an explicit, recorded agreement, which that proposal removes.

1. Summary

  1. Telegram's own rules require explicit consent for what Muallim does. The Bot Platform developer terms prohibit "any form of data collection aimed at creating large datasets, machine learning models" and AI products. Data that users submit themselves may be used only if they are told clearly how and give "individual, explicit, active and revocable consent". Building a dataset from voice messages without such consent risks the bot being banned.
  2. The proposal to drop the consent screen (ADR-0085) cannot be combined with a public dataset. A notice line without a button is not "explicit" or "active". The author's newer requirement — the learner presses «Продолжить» after being told the recordings become public — is the right direction.
  3. A voice cannot be made anonymous, and a published copy cannot be recalled. The consent text must say both in plain words. No comparable dataset found in Phase 2 documents its consent; doing so is part of Muallim's contribution.
  4. Where the server stands is a legal question, not only a technical one. Commentary on Kazakhstan's law says personal data must be stored on servers in Kazakhstan; commentary on Russia's law says first collection of Russian citizens' data must go to a database in Russia. Publishing on Hugging Face is a cross-border transfer on top of that. The project's open question OQ-08 (where to host) decides which rules apply.
  5. Recommended consent design: one clear screen before the first recording; «Продолжить» means agreement to public release; a second, smaller option lets a person practise without sharing. Learners under 18 use the bot but their recordings are not published.
  6. Recommended licence design: gated access on Hugging Face with usage terms that forbid identifying speakers, cloning voices and redistributing the audio, and oblige users to honour removals.
  7. Recommended architecture: keep the existing layered design; add a second, independent "verifier" step and a shadow mode so that several recognisers can be compared on the same attempts without changing what the learner sees.

8.3 Rules the code must enforce

  1. No audio is written to disk before a public_release consent exists.
  2. An attempt is publishable only if the consent was valid when it was recorded; later consent does not cover earlier attempts unless the learner says so.
  3. A new consent text version does not carry over old agreement where the meaning changed; the learner is asked again.
  4. Age group "under 18" or unanswered → never publishable.
  5. Withdrawal: stop recording at once; delete internal audio and its labels (existing behaviour); add the speaker to the removal list; the next release omits them.
  6. Every release is built from scratch from current consents, never by editing the previous one.
  7. Consent events stay in the database and out of process logs (existing rule).

1. Summary

  1. There are ten distinguishable ways to build such a system, from a human teacher on a video call to a live voice model that talks back. Seven of them use speech technology (section 3).
  2. All automatic systems share the same five building blocks: text → expected sounds; a model that listens; a comparison with what was expected; a decision about what to tell the learner; and the feedback itself. Products differ mainly in the middle three (section 4).
  3. The market leader checks words, not sounds. Tarteel follows recitation and flags missed or wrong words; letter-level detection was named only as a future goal when the feature launched, and an independent review found it unable to detect vowel-sign errors.
  4. Products that claim sound-level tajwid feedback exist, but none publishes an independent evaluation. The one independent review found (2025) rated all four AI apps it tested as unreliable.
  5. The oldest system is still the most instructive. HAFSS (2006) already had three ideas modern apps lack: it listens only for expected kinds of error; it has a confidence score with four possible responses, including "say it again"; and it can restrict itself to vowel errors for beginners.
  6. Almost nothing targets Muallim's first stage. Products start from words or verses. The systems that handle single letters are either research prototypes or children's apps whose own description says they give "recognized-word feedback".
  7. No system found works inside Telegram, and none documents consent for the voice it collects.
  8. For Muallim: its present design (sound-level model without a language model, compared with a reference) belongs to the strongest family. What it lacks compared with the best examples is a decision policy — when to stay silent, when to ask for a repeat — and a restricted list of errors per lesson.

6.1 HAFSS (RDI and Cairo University, 2006) — commercial product for non-native learners

Part How it works
Unit of analysis Not the phoneme but the unit of traditional teaching: consonant + short vowel, consonant + long vowel, bare consonant, and their doubled forms. Chosen because "most Qur'an learners are not familiar with phonemes"
Expected text A transcription engine reads the Quran text and produces the correct sound sequence with recitation rules and their exceptions
Error hypotheses A database of 663 rules of typical recitation errors. Each rule says: if the current unit, the previous and the next look like this, add this wrong variant. The result is a lattice of the correct reading plus probable wrong ones
Recognition The recogniser may only choose among the paths in the lattice. It does not transcribe freely
Restriction per lesson A subset of rules can be switched on — the paper's example: "vowel type" errors only, "for a realization dedicated to beginners"
Speaker adaptation The acoustic model adapts to each user's voice
Durations A separate layer checks lengths, with normalisation for the reader's speed
Confidence A likelihood ratio between the chosen path and the next best
Four possible responses 1) say nothing — "good for novice users because reporting false alarms discourages them"; 2) ask to repeat; 3) report an unidentified error and ask to repeat; 4) report the most probable error
Feedback Each error rule is linked to a colour code, a text message, an audio message and the relevant recitation rule
Reported result On 507 utterances labelled by experts, 6.6 % of segments were wrong. Of those errors the system identified 62.4 %, asked for a repeat on 22.4 % and wrongly accepted 14.9 %. On correct speech it asked for a repeat in 5.1 % of utterances

Source: Abdou et al., Interspeech 2006 [P]. The 2025 review notes the system was not widely adopted in twenty years and questions its test population. [P]

6.3 Checking memorisation from transcripts

The supervisor's group studied a production checker that works on ASR transcripts of memorisation recordings (pattern 3). Findings relevant to design: [P] abstract, arXiv:2609.12085

  • A plain word-by-word difference with the reference already locates most events (localisation F1 0.83) but labels them poorly (0.53).
  • The hard part is not detection but conventions: repetitions, self-repairs, opening formulas and accepted spelling variants must not count as mistakes. Ten labels were needed.

8. What I noticed

  1. The field moved from "listen only for expected errors" to "write down everything and compare" — and lost the decision policy on the way. HAFSS decided what to say using confidence and the learner's level. Most modern open models stop at the sound sequence and leave the decision to the app. Quran Lab says so explicitly: "The SDK is an inference engine."
  2. Vendors' claims and the one independent review point in opposite directions. Marketing describes real-time sound-level correction; the review found the tested apps unreliable, and noted that the market leader recognises words rather than judging sounds. Neither side publishes test data.
  3. "Sound-level feedback" in children's apps is often derived from word recognition. Amal's own page says so. A wrong word tells the app which sound to show a tip for; it does not mean the sound was heard as wrong.
  4. Three products compare the learner with a master reciter's voice. The review's criticism of this is specific: it rewards imitation of a voice and treats natural variation as error.
  5. Everyone needs the text-to-sounds engine, and for the Quran several good ones are open. For non-Quran vowelled text (a primer's syllables and words) the tools are fewer; the bot already has its own solution.
  6. Speed normalisation appears twice, twenty years apart (HAFSS, HisnLabs). Any judgement of length needs the reader's own pace, which matters even more for slow beginners.
  7. Self-correction and repetition are a first-class problem in the memorisation checker and in the HisnLabs demo ("self-corrected" as a word state). The project already treats self-correction separately.
  8. Running on the phone is used as a privacy argument (Google, TajweedMate, Quran Lab). A Telegram bot cannot do this; it must rely on consent instead (Phase 5).
  9. Beginners are named as a user group but not served by design. Only HAFSS mentions restricting errors for beginners, and only as a possibility.
  10. The 2025 review argues for building on rules and articulation knowledge rather than on data alone. Its target is the same weakness Phase 1 called "hears the rule, not the learner", seen from the other side: data-driven models inherit the bias of their data.

9. What this means for Muallim

Where Muallim stands

Muallim is in family 5: a sound-level model without a language model, comparison with a reference, rules that name the error and an explanation in Russian. This is the family with the best benchmark evidence. In two respects it is ahead of the products reviewed: it stores the full trace of each decision, and it has teacher labelling built in.

What not to copy

  • Word recognition as the judge of pronunciation (Tarteel, Amal): it cannot see vowel errors.
  • A single master reciter as the yardstick.
  • A general voice model as the teacher: no evidence, per-minute cost, and the learner cannot verify what it says.
  • Claims of accuracy without a published test: the review shows how this is read by experts.

Gaps Muallim could fill

  • A documented, evaluated system for the letters-and-vowels stage for adult non-Arab beginners.
  • A published decision policy (when the system stays silent) with measured false-alarm rates.
  • Delivery through a messenger, with documented consent.
  • An evaluation against teachers that others can reproduce — the thing every vendor lacks.

1. Summary

  1. The same three designs appear everywhere. Products either recognise words and compare them with the text, or score each expected sound against a model of native speech, or let a general voice model talk to the learner. No fourth design was found in any language.
  2. The closest relatives of Muallim's first stage are children's reading tools, not adult language apps. They face the same task: one letter sound, said alone, by someone who cannot read yet. The only published accuracy for it found here is 85 % agreement with human raters on letter sounds, against 95 % between two humans (2011).
  3. Vendors of children's tools say openly that isolated sounds are the hard case. Sounds said alone lack the context that helps recognition inside words. This matches our own test, where no system exceeded 50 % on single vowelled letters.
  4. Commercial scoring services do not cover Arabic. Speechace lists English, French and Spanish; SpeechSuper lists eight languages without Arabic. Microsoft's service lists Arabic but returned no sound labels in a 2025 report (Phase 1).
  5. Independent evidence is as thin abroad as in Arabic. Accuracy claims are vendors' own. A 2025 systematic review of thirty studies states that such systems "still struggle to accurately evaluate" learner speech and that most research measured results with human listeners, not with the software.
  6. What is well supported is the teaching effect, not the scoring accuracy. Meta-analyses find that computer pronunciation training helps, most of all beginners and most of all with explicit feedback on single sounds.
  7. Nobody found works the way Muallim does: a graded textbook from single letters, checked by voice in a messenger, with teachers verifying the labels. A few hobby Telegram bots exist for English; none publishes an evaluation.

Ngo, Chen and Lai (2024), meta-analysis of 15 studies, cited in the review [P] Recognition-based training improves single sounds more than stress and intonation; explicit feedback is the most effective

Meaning for Muallim: the learners (beginners), the target (single sounds) and the feedback style (explicit: what was expected, what was heard) are the combination for which the literature reports the largest benefit.

10. What not to borrow

  • A single pass-or-fail without saying what was wrong (Rosetta Stone, Duolingo): users cannot tell 60 % from 95 %.
  • Accuracy stated as one headline figure without method (ELSA).
  • Word recognition as proof of pronunciation (Duolingo).

11. Limitations

  • Survey depth: most entries rest on search summaries, not on the vendors' pages themselves.
  • No product was tried. Chinese-language sources on the Putonghua test and on the large Chinese vendors were not searched.
  • Prices, user numbers and market size were not collected.
  • Japanese, Korean and Indian-language tools were not examined separately.
  • The list of children's tools is English-centred.
